# AI Automation Agency vs In-House Hire: Cost, Speed and When Each Makes Sense

## Overview

By Mikhail Oskola, founder of Retailbox Automation. Checked: salary figures as published by the trackers named in the text, dated 31 August to 3 September 2026. An AI automation agency and an in-house automation hire solve the same problem two different ways. One you pay per project or per month; the other you put on payroll and manage yourself. An agency usually costs less upfront and can start sooner, because there is no hiring step; an in-house hire costs more from day one but gives you someone embedded in the business full-time, with no vendor to coordinate once they're ramped. We're an agency, so we have a stake in this answer. Neither is the default right answer. It depends on how much ongoing automation work you actually have, and whether that work is steady enough to justify a full-time salary. This breaks down the real cost difference, when each option makes sense, and why companies past a certain size often end up running both at once. Agency vs. in-house hire: what's actually different? An AI automation agency is a team you hire per project or on a monthly retainer. A good one has built workflows like yours before for other companies; check that in its references, not in its pitch. The catch: you're sharing their attention with other clients, and the relationship ends, or continues on a retainer, when the project does. An in-house automation hire is one person (or a small team) on your payroll, full-time, sitting in your meetings and your Slack. They know your business, your data, and your stack better than any outside team ever will. But they cost a full salary whether there's automation work that week or not, and if they leave, most of what they knew leaves with them unless it was documented. Neither option is automatically cheaper or more serious than the other. The right call depends on how much steady automation work actually exists, not on which one sounds more permanent. A single CRM migration or one automation project doesn't clear that bar. An agency finishes it and moves on, and you're not carrying a salary through the months between initiatives. A company running dozens of workflows that need constant tuning, new integrations as tools get added, and someone who can drop everything when one breaks starts looking more like a full-time job. When does an in-house hire or AI team make sense? An in-house automation hire earns their salary once there's enough automation and integration work to fill a full-time role year-round. Not one project, but a continuous backlog: new integrations as the company adds tools, workflows that need rebuilding as the business changes, and enough volume that someone sitting in daily standups catches problems before they get expensive. It also makes sense when the work touches something sensitive enough that having it built by someone who reports to you, not a vendor, is worth the premium: regulated data, or a process nobody outside the company should see the internals of. The tradeoff is ramp time: even a strong senior hire needs weeks to learn your CRM, your data model, and which systems are actually load-bearing before they ship anything real. When does an agency or specialist consultancy make more sense? An agency makes more sense when the automation need is real but doesn't add up to a full-time job: one CRM implementation, a handful of workflows, an AI agent for one specific process. If the agency has built something similar before, it isn't learning on your time, and you're not carrying a six-figure salary through the months when there's nothing to build. It's also the lower-risk starting point if you're not sure automation will be a permanent function in the company yet. Run one project, see the payback, and decide whether it's worth building a team around before committing to a hire. Can an agency work alongside an in-house ops person? Yes, and it's a common setup past a certain size. An in-house ops or RevOps person who owns the day-to-day and knows the business, backed by an agency for the specialized builds a generalist can't take on alone (a CRM migration, an AI agent, anything outside what one person can build and still do their regular job). The in-house person doesn't need to be a full automation engineer to make this work; they need to know what's running, flag what's breaking, and be the point of contact when an outside project needs internal context. What does an in-house automation hire actually cost? US automation engineer salaries sit in a fairly tight band across the major trackers. As of September 2026: Salary.com puts the national average at $108,677 (as of September 1, 2026), Indeed at $108,033 (updated August 31, 2026), ZipRecruiter at $107,126 (as of September 3, 2026), and Glassdoor higher at $119,731. Built In puts engineers with 7+ years of experience at $131,421 on average. One caveat on all of these: "automation engineer" is a broad job title. The same salary data also covers QA test-automation engineers and industrial or manufacturing automation roles, so it's a reasonable benchmark for a business-process automation hire, not an exact match. None of those figures is the full cost. Benefits and payroll overhead are commonly estimated at another 25-40% on top of base salary. Applied to the $107,000-$120,000 base range above, that puts a fully loaded in-house automation hire at about $134,000-$168,000 a year, before they've shipped a single workflow. If there's enough steady automation work to keep one person building or maintaining something every month, the salary is the better buy. If there isn't, project work costs less.
