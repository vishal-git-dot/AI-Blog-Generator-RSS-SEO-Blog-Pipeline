# When everyone has a private screen, how do values still get passed on?

## Overview

A six-year-old sits on the floor of a Sudanese home, inside Sudan or in one of the cities people fled to. He is holding his mother's phone. On the screen, a monkey made by AI wears a jalabiya and a turban. It stands in a market that looks like the one near his house, and it speaks the dialect of his family. Then it ends its line with an insult. The boy laughs. He says the line again in his own voice. In the evening the phone passes to his older brother, home from school. I should be clear about this scene. It is a composite. It does not describe one child, and it does not prove that any child copied anything. It brings together pieces that kept repeating in what I watched over many months. I am not writing from outside. I watch this content too. The scroll takes me the way it takes everyone, and more than once I laughed before I noticed what I was laughing at. The screen we used to share Not long ago, much of what reached people on television and radio came in shared programmes at shared times. The set stood in the main room. The family watched together, and what was shown became something to talk about at home and in public. That era had its censorship and its bias. But it gave people a common reference. You could point to a programme and criticise it, because everyone had seen the same thing. Today a growing share of what we watch comes through personal feeds. Each of us gets what matches our interests, what we tapped, what we lingered on. Two brothers in one house can live two media lives with almost nothing in common. So can a child in Khartoum and a child in Milan. I am not saying shared culture has disappeared. I am saying that what people see, how it reaches them, and whether anyone can question it in public have all changed. The signs are not local. Oxford chose "brain rot" as its word of the year for 2024, defined as the supposed decline of a person's mental state from overconsuming trivial online content. In Italy, hybrid AI-generated animal characters spread on TikTok with engagement well above the usual. None of this proves that all generated content is bad, or that high view counts mean harm. It does raise a question worth checking: what happens when cheap production meets recommendation systems and engagement incentives, in places where moderation understands the language less well? To think about that question, I needed two words. Moral desertification I borrowed the first word from environmental geography. Land does not become desert in a day. It loses its cover and its fertility slowly, until it stops growing anything. By moral desertification I mean a possible, gradual weakening of the social conditions in which values are learned and passed between generations. It happens, if it happens, when people are repeatedly exposed to content that puts the unacceptable in the place of reward, in feeds that adults do not see, from models that have no face. The concept does not claim that a society has "lost" its values or that a generation is "corrupt". It describes a path, and conditions along that path, some of which can be observed. I tie it to three markers: Normalising the unacceptable. Insults, contempt and stigma repeat inside laughter until they lose their weight. This one can be observed in the content itself. The role model moves. It shifts from a person with a face and a name to a generated character whose owner nobody knows. The conditions for this can be seen in the content. Whether the shift happens in children needs audience research. Crossing borders. The same template appears in neighbouring countries and dialects. This needs cross-border samples. Liquid knowledge The second word builds on Zygmunt Bauman's idea of liquid modernity, where nothing keeps its shape for long. It also meets what Byung-Chul Han calls infocracy, where fast information pushes out narrative. By liquid knowledge I mean short-lived units, without a source and without context, that take the place of knowledge that used to travel through the story, the proverb, the gathering and the book. They are easy to consume and hard to build into understanding. Short does not mean shallow, and long does not mean deep. Shortness is a property of the format. Shallow consumption is a property of how we receive it. Lost context is a property of the source. I call knowledge liquid when several of these come together: no source, a unit that could be swapped for any other template, a catchphrase that stays in memory while the meaning does not, and nothing practised after watching. In the model I propose, liquid knowledge is the candidate mechanism. Desertification is the possible outcome. What this is, and what it is not This is a preliminary conceptual framework, not a tested theory. The links between its parts are hypotheses, not proven causes. It is also not a moral panic. Every new medium has been blamed for ruining the young, and the research on those fears tends to start from zero each time. I am not blaming short video or the people who make it. A framework like this has to name what would prove it wrong. If systematic samples show the pattern is rare, if exposure turns out to have no link to change in children, or if families and schools absorb the effect, the concept weakens. The sample that started it Since April 2026, whenever I came across one of these clips on TikTok during my normal use, I downloaded it to my phone. The rule for inclusion was simple: AI-generated animals with Sudanese human features and clothes, speaking Sudanese Arabic. That gave me 34 public videos. It is an incidental, purposive sample, shaped by my own feed and by what caught my eye. Coded conservatively, 23 of them (68%) contained at least one objectionable element, such as insults, sexual content, harassment played for laughs, mockery of women as women, or tribal and regional stigma. In 12 of them (35%), TikTok's AI-generated label was visible in the frames I examined. These figures describe the sample. They do not measure how common this content is on the platform, and they say nothing about effects on children. The same sample also argues against easy conclusions: 11 videos carried nothing objectionable in my coding. The format does not decide the content. Where this series goes I wrote this up as a preprint, and I will walk through it in six parts: This one: the two concepts. How one private feed leaks into a whole family through a shared phone. Why a role model without a face may teach behaviour more easily than values. What I found in the 34 videos, and where my second coder and I disagreed. Harm in a dialect that moderation systems cannot read, and the incentives behind it. From protection to what I call knowledge cultivation: design principles and who should do what. The full paper, in Arabic with an English abstract, is here: Moral Desertification and Liquid Knowledge in the Algorithmic Age (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24333671).
