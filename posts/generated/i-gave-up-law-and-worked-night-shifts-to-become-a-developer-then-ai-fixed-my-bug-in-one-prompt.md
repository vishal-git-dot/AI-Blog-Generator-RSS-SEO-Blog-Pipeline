# I Gave Up Law and Worked Night Shifts to Become a Developer. Then AI Fixed My Bug in One Prompt.

## Overview

I gave Claude Code a bug. Not a cute one. The kind that eats an entire day: reproduce it, dig through logs, trace it across five files, try three wrong fixes before the right one. One prompt later, it was fixed. I sat there staring at the diff like it had insulted my family. And then a very quiet, very unpleasant thought showed up: Did I do all of that for nothing? 😶 To explain why that thought hurt so much, I have to rewind to a law school, a bank, and a lot of night shifts. Eight hours of plans. Zero needed. I was supposed to be a lawyer In my last year of law school I interned at Citibank. On paper it was legal and compliance work. In practice I kept drifting toward the software side of things: the systems, the tools, the people who built them. I got curious, and the curiosity refused to leave. I finished my law degree anyway. I'd come that far. Then, in 2019, I moved to the US for a job that had nothing to do with tech, and the question came with me in my suitcase. Day shift, night shift I took CS50 online and later joined a coding bootcamp. Good friends gave me my first real chance: a role at their small startup, building React and React Native features and learning by shipping to actual users. It did not pay the bills. So my schedule looked like this: Daytime: writing code at the startup Nighttime: a warehouse shift, and later a night shift at Ross Somewhere in between: sleep, in theory 🙃 Two jobs, one hoodie. The hardest part wasn't the code. It was believing it would eventually turn into a "real" developer job, when everything about my résumé said "lawyer who stocks shelves." The night shift ends In 2021 I got my first full-time engineering role at a consultancy, building React and Node apps for big enterprise clients. I quit the night shifts. 💃 Within a year I was leading a small UI team and running incident response for a global retailer's inventory system. A few years earlier I'd been reading contracts. From there: a privacy-focused ML startup, where I was the only frontend engineer. Then the senior role I have now, where most of my work is AI: LLM integrations, evaluation, and keeping model costs from exploding. I'd made it. Which, as you already know from the first paragraph, is exactly when the universe sent a bug fix. The very bad thought Back to that diff. The law degree I walked away from. The bootcamp. The warehouse. Ross. Learning React at 2 a.m. on almost no sleep. Years of grinding to get into a profession, and just as I got comfortable, a machine was doing in seconds the thing I'd fought so hard to learn. I'd already switched careers once. I wasn't sure I had a second switch in me. What pulled me out of it What helped was remembering something I'd already lived through. Before Claude Code existed, I built the UI for a product that was applying to Y Combinator. I used AI to help, and I shipped it in a matter of days . A few years earlier the same work would have taken me months. The application ranked in the top 10% of Y Combinator applicants. AI didn't build that product. I did, faster than I could have alone. I decided what to build, what "good" looked like, and when the AI's output was wrong. Spoiler: it was wrong plenty. Very fast. Very confident. Occasionally correct. So instead of running from AI, I made it my job. The more time I spent with these models, the less scared I got. They're incredibly fast. They're also confidently wrong in ways that are easy to miss if you don't know what you're looking at. They don't know what your users need. They don't notice when "working" code quietly does the wrong thing. Someone has to. Turns out that someone is me. These days I build open-source tools around AI, like TwoPass , which double-checks cheap batch LLM output so you don't pay for the expensive model on every call. My whole approach fits in one line: an agent on autopilot is a liability; an agent supervised by someone who understands the code is a superpower. So, was it all for nothing? No. Everything I learned the hard way is exactly what lets me use AI well instead of being replaced by it. I can read the code it writes and know when it's wrong. I know what "done" means, because I've been on incident duty when it wasn't. Even law school, the thing I thought I'd wasted, turned out to matter. Lawyers are trained to read carefully, question every assumption, and hunt for the one clause that breaks the argument. That is precisely what reviewing AI-generated code demands. 🧐 Turns out code review is just contract law with semicolons. The job didn't disappear. It changed shape: less typing, more judging. And judgment is the part you can't skip your way into. Could someone make my switch today? I think yes, but differently. The beginner tasks I learned on are the ones AI handles best now, so the first rung feels harder to reach. But today's career changer also has a tutor that never sleeps and never gets tired of "why?" Use it to understand , not to skip understanding, and you'll learn faster than I ever could. If you're where I was Your "irrelevant" background isn't irrelevant. Find the part that transfers. Mine was careful reading and skepticism. Ship real things early, even unpaid, even small. The startup work mattered more than any certificate. Use AI as a teacher, not a ghostwriter. Ask it to explain, write it yourself, then ask it to review you. Do I regret not becoming a lawyer? Honestly? It's a hard question. Sometimes I do. But mostly I don't. The market feels rough right now, and every day there's news of another round of layoffs. Even so, tech still feels full of possibility to me. There's still room to be creative, to build something out of nothing and put it in front of people. And the scariest thing I've learned is also the most comforting: the night shift ends. Even the one AI put me on. 🌅 Clocking out. For good.
