# 15 Web & Cloud Terms Every Non-Technical Founder Should Actually Understand

## Overview

Ever nod along in a meeting when someone says "we need to sort out the CDN before we scale the backend," and quietly hope nobody asks you to explain what that means? You're not alone. I'm going to explain 15 terms that come up constantly when a business builds or scales a website, app, or SaaS product — without the academic definitions nobody asked for. If you already know these, this isn't for you. If you don't, and you're tired of pretending you do in client calls, keep reading. To make it stick, let's follow a small business owner through her first year of going digital. Her name is Nadia , and she runs a bakery called Nadia's Sourdough . She's excellent at bread. She is not, by her own admission, excellent at "the internet stuff." Let's go. 1. Domain Name What is it? The address people type to find your website. nadiassourdough.com , not the string of numbers the internet actually uses behind the scenes. Nadia bought her domain in about four minutes and then spent three days trying to decide between .com and .co.nz . Completely normal. Nobody warns you that the domain name decision takes longer than naming your firstborn. 2. Hosting What is it? The computer (usually a server sitting in a data centre somewhere) that actually stores your website's files and serves them to visitors. Your domain name is the address; hosting is the building the address points to. Nadia initially assumed "hosting" meant something to do with catering. It does not. Though to be fair, bad hosting can leave your site as unavailable as a bakery that's run out of bread. 3. SSL Certificate What is it? The thing that turns http:// into https:// and gives visitors that little padlock icon. It encrypts data between your site and your visitor, so nobody can snoop on it mid-transit. Nadia didn't care about this until her web developer mentioned browsers actively warn visitors away from sites without one. Suddenly she cared a great deal. 4. CMS (Content Management System) What is it? Software that lets you edit your own website — add a blog post, change a price, swap a photo — without touching code. WordPress is the most common one. Nadia can now update her menu herself instead of emailing her developer every time she adds a new sourdough flavour. This single feature has probably saved more client relationships than any other item on this list. 5. UI vs UX What is it? UI (User Interface) is what something looks like — buttons, colours, layout. UX (User Experience) is how it feels to use — is it confusing, is it fast, does the checkout button hide where nobody can find it. You can have gorgeous UI and terrible UX. Nadia's first site had a beautiful hero image and an "Order Now" button that, when clicked, did absolutely nothing. Great UI. Catastrophic UX. 6. Front-End vs Back-End What is it? Front-end is everything the visitor sees and clicks on. Back-end is everything happening on the server that they don't see — processing the order, checking stock, charging the card. Nadia's front-end shows a nice photo of a cinnamon loaf. Her back-end quietly checks whether she actually has any cinnamon loaves left before letting someone pay for one. When that check doesn't exist, you get the timeless business classic: selling bread you don't have. 7. API What is it? A defined way for two pieces of software to talk to each other. Instead of Nadia's ordering system rebuilding a payment processor from scratch, it just asks Stripe's API to "please charge this card," and Stripe handles the scary part. APIs are why nobody has to reinvent GPS, weather data, or credit card processing every time they build something new. Everyone just asks the specialist. 8. Full-Stack Development What is it? Working across both the front-end and back-end of a project, rather than specialising in just one. When Nadia's ordering site needed a nicer menu page and a fix for the stock-checking bug in the same week, a full-stack developer handled both without a handoff meeting. Efficient, if slightly less glamorous-sounding than "I specialise exclusively in buttons." 9. Database What is it? Where your actual data lives — customer orders, stock levels, account details — structured so it can be searched and updated quickly. Nadia's database currently contains 40 customer names, 6 bread types, and one entry for "Gary — allergic to literally everything, call before delivering." Small, but sacred. 10. Cloud Hosting What is it? Instead of your site living on one physical server that falls over the moment it gets popular, cloud hosting spreads the load across many machines that can scale up automatically when traffic spikes. Nadia's site got mentioned by a local food blogger and traffic tripled overnight. Because she was on cloud hosting, the site scaled with it. Her cousin's site, hosted on a single ageing server under someone's desk, did not survive its own viral moment quite so gracefully. 11. CDN (Content Delivery Network) What is it? A network of servers around the world that cache copies of your site so it loads fast no matter where the visitor is, instead of every request travelling back to one server on the other side of the planet. Nadia's cousin in London complained the site "felt slow." Once a CDN was added, it stopped feeling like her photos of sourdough were travelling by actual ship. 12. SaaS (Software as a Service) What is it? Software you access online and pay for on subscription, rather than installing and owning outright. Think an ongoing rental, not a one-time purchase. Nadia doesn't run a SaaS herself, but she uses about six of them daily without noticing: her booking calendar, her accounting software, her email tool. Modern small business, held together entirely by other people's monthly subscriptions. 13. Multi-Tenancy What is it? The architecture that lets one SaaS platform serve many different customers ("tenants") from the same underlying system, while keeping each customer's data completely separate. The booking calendar Nadia uses serves thousands of businesses from one platform. She sees her own bookings. She does not see her competitor's, or anyone else's — that separation is the entire point, and it's much harder to build properly than it sounds. 14. CI/CD (Continuous Integration / Continuous Deployment) What is it? An automated pipeline that tests and ships code changes without a human manually copying files onto a server and hoping for the best. When Nadia's developer fixed the stock-checking bug, CI/CD tested the fix automatically and deployed it within minutes. In a world without CI/CD, that fix waits for someone to remember to upload it manually, usually right before they go on holiday. 15. Bounce Rate What is it? The percentage of visitors who land on your site and leave without doing anything else — no clicks, no scrolling, nothing. Nadia's bounce rate dropped noticeably after fixing the broken "Order Now" button from item #5. Turns out people leave quickly when the thing they came to do doesn't work. Genuinely shocking research findings. That's 15. There's a longer list I could've kept going with — hosting uptime, load balancers, DNS propagation, the eternal mystery of why cache clearing fixes everything — but fifteen is already a lot to absorb before lunch. If you're a founder who's been quietly Googling half these terms during client calls: you're further ahead than you think, and now you at least have the vocabulary to ask better questions of whoever's building your site. What term took you the longest to actually understand? Mine was CDN — I spent an embarrassingly long time assuming it was just a fancier word for hosting.
