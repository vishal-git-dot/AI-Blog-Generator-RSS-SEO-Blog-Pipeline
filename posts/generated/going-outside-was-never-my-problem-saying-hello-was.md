# Going outside was never my problem. Saying hello was.

## Overview

This is a submission for the Hacktoberfest Open-Source AI Challenge Week 1: Touch Grass What I Built I'm an introvert by nature but an extrovert by society . In a meeting or anything official, I'm fine, mainly because there's a reason to talk. What I struggle with is talking to people when I don't have to, or what we call small talk, especially with someone I don't necessarily need to talk to. So when the theme was "Touch Grass", I didn't want another app that tells you to go for a walk. Going outside isn't the hard part for introverts. Talking to the people out there is . Many social apps help you talk to people, but almost all of them keep you talking through a screen. This app is the opposite. It gets you talking to the world around you: the people you already pass every day and never really speak to. It’s about realizing that every random passerby is living a life just as vivid, complex, and emotionally rich as your own . That’s the idea behind the app. Sonder is a weekly game for that. It makes it easier to connect with the people around you, without the pressure of walking up to a stranger and figuring out what to say. Because sometimes, all it takes to turn a stranger into a friend is a little nudge. Every week you get a few quests on sticky notes set in places you actually go . It comes with two difficulty levels (easy, fine, and scary) You do the quest out in the world, come back, and write what you found out . Gemma replies like a friend, and your notes build into a wall of people who aren't strangers anymore. It's for anyone who walks past the same faces every day and never says more than "good morning". How it works Tell it your normal week. Pick the places you actually go (market, office, gym, bus stop, church or mosque, beach and more), or add your own. Pick your challenge. Easy, Fine or Scary (see below). Get your week. Gemma writes a mix of quests for your places. Each has a one-line opener you can actually say. Go and do them. Write down what you found out. Gemma replies to what you learned, not to the quest. Watch your wall grow. The People tab keeps every note, week by week. Settings let you change places, level, and quests per week at any time. New quests swaps the ones you haven't started and keeps the ones you've done. Three levels Level What a quest looks like Example Easy One quick question, under a minute Ask someone at the bus stop which bus goes somewhere you know Fine A light chat with one simple follow-up Ask a regular at the gym to show you one exercise they swear by Scary A real conversation Ask an older trader at the market what has changed most over the years Every week mixes quest types , so no two weeks feel the same. There are 14 types, from Learn a name and Ask the way up to Origin story and Return visit (going back to someone you met before). How I Built It The stack is small on purpose: Gemma, running locally through Ollama FastAPI and SQLite on my laptop One HTML page for the whole interface, opened on my phone. I made it whimsical and delightful The interesting part wasn't the stack. It was getting a small model to write quests worth doing. What Gemma does Writes your week. It turns your places and your challenge level into a set of quests, each with an opener you can actually say and, for Fine and Scary, a follow-up question. Replies to what you found out. When you finish a quest and write a note, Gemma responds to the person you met, like a friend would over text. How the app is built around a small local model Small models are fast and private, but they copy examples and drift off-brief. So the app gives Gemma structure instead of freedom: Each week is planned before Gemma writes anything. The app splits the week into slots, and each slot gets a quest type (14 types, from "Learn a name" to "Origin story") and one of your places. Gemma writes one quest per slot, so the week stays varied and tied to where you actually go. Gemma returns structured JSON. Ollama's structured output means every quest arrives with the same fields: task, opener, follow-up, and place. Every quest is checked in code before you see it. It's dropped if it's a label instead of a task, small talk, a copy of its own brief, too long to say out loud, set somewhere you didn't pick, or a repeat. Safety rules are enforced in code, not just in the prompt: never asks about marriage, tribe, politics, money, or age, and never asks for phone numbers or photos no faith questions at all hospital quests are staff-only no alcohol at bars or drink shops a "Play it safe" mode keeps everything in shops and queues 83 backup quests fill any gaps, so a bad run from the model never leaves you with an empty week. Weeks are written in the background. The phone checks back every few seconds, so a slow model never freezes the app. Why Does Open Innovation Matter? Because of what Sonder stores. It's a diary of every person you talked to, what they told you, and which conversations you skipped because they felt awkward or unsafe. I wouldn't send that to a server I don't control, and I don't think anyone else should either. That's also why there's no hosted version: the moment I host it, your notes live on my server. With an open model running locally: Nothing leaves my laptop. Gemma, the quests, and the notes all live on my machine. It costs nothing to run. That matters in Nigeria, where paying per request adds up fast. I could choose my own speed and quality trade-off. I tried gemma3:1b, which wrote a week in about 2 seconds but gave plainer quests. I went back to gemma3:4b for better ones, and kept it loaded in memory so it's ready when you open the app. With a closed API, that choice wouldn't be mine. The safety filters are in my code, and anyone can read them in the repo. My Agent Session I don't have a DevRelay session to share. I didn't build Sonder with Claude Code.
