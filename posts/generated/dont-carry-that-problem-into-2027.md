Why some business problems survive a whole year When a problem has lasted twelve months, it's rarely because it's technically difficult. In our experience it survives for one of three reasons. 1. It was written down as a solution "We need a new CRM." "We need an app." "We need AI." Each of these describes a purchase, not a problem. Once a problem is phrased as a product, every conversation turns into a vendor comparison, and nobody checks whether the product would have fixed anything. 2. Its cost is spread thin Ten minutes of re-keying here, a status meeting there, a spreadsheet someone rebuilds every Friday afternoon. No single loss is big enough to force a decision, but together they add up. Asana's Anatomy of Work research, based on a survey of more than 10,000 knowledge workers, found that people spend about 60% of their time on "work about work": chasing updates, searching for information and switching between apps. Very little of that shows up as a line in anyone's budget, so very little of it gets fixed. 3. It looks too big to start A problem that seems to need a big project waits for a big budget and a quiet quarter. Neither arrives. Yet small projects are the ones that work. The Standish Group has tracked software project outcomes since 1994, and its 2015 CHAOS data found small projects were at least ten times more likely to succeed than large ones. So the job is to make the problem specific, put a price on it, and shrink it to something you can start. The five steps below do that. Step 1: Define the problem without naming a technology Clear problem definition is the foundation of any business process improvement for small business. Skip it and every later decision rests on a guess. Write a one-sentence problem statement Use this template: [Who] spends [time or money] on [what] because [cause], and the result is [consequence]. Example: Our three account managers each spend about six hours a week copying orders from email into the ERP, because the web store and the ERP don't share data, and the result is shipping errors that cost us repeat customers. This sentence doesn't mention a CRM, AI, a platform or an app, and that's deliberate. If you can't write the sentence without those words, you're still describing a solution. Use the 5 Whys to find the root cause The 5 Whys is a root cause analysis technique that came out of Toyota's production system and sits at the center of lean thinking. You state the problem, ask why it happens, then ask why again about each answer. Five rounds is a guide, not a rule. You stop when you reach a cause you can act on. Question Answer What's the problem? "We need a new CRM." Why? Sales can't see where an order is. Why? Order status lives in the ERP, and sales works in the CRM. Why does that matter? Someone copies updates between them by hand. Why is that a problem? It happens late or not at all, so customers get wrong answers. Root cause The two systems don't share order status. The business doesn't need a new CRM. It needs two systems that share one piece of data. That fix can cost a fraction of a CRM migration and take weeks instead of months. Get agreement before moving on Read the sentence to the people who live with the problem every day. If they say "that's it, exactly," move to step 2. If they start correcting you, you've just saved yourself from fixing the wrong thing. Step 2: Calculate what the problem costs A problem with a cost attached gets decided. A problem without one gets discussed. As a rule of thumb, if the fix costs more than one or two years of the problem, look for a smaller fix. You won't get the number exactly right, and you don't need to. A cautious estimate your team believes is worth more than a precise one nobody trusts. Measuring the cost of manual data entry this way also gives you a baseline to prove the fix worked later. Step 3: Choose the lightest fix that will hold Most leaders jump straight to building or buying something. Climb this ladder instead, and stop at the first step that solves the problem. Step Fix When it fits Typical signal 1 Remove the step The work adds no value Reports nobody reads, approvals nobody refuses 2 Set up what you already own Your current tools can do it Features you pay for but don't use 3 Connect your systems Data exists but sits in the wrong place The same data typed into two or more tools 4 Automate the workflow The process is right but repetitive Copy, paste, check, send, repeat 5 Build custom software Your workflow really differs from what tools assume Workarounds stacked on workarounds 6 Add people What's missing is skill or capacity The right process with nobody to run it Why you start at the bottom Lean thinking says to remove waste before you automate it. Automating a broken process just produces the wrong result faster. Each step up the ladder costs more and takes longer, so the lowest step that holds is almost always the best value. System integration for small business: an example A logistics client of ours ran six platforms that didn't talk to each other. Staff spent more than 20 hours a week on manual entry, and fulfillment errors were costly. The fix connected the six systems instead of adding a seventh. Manual data entry fell by 73%, fulfillment errors dropped close to zero, and the business now saves about $3 million a year in operating costs. That was step three. If your team types the same data into more than one tool, connecting systems instead of replacing them is often the lighter fix. Workflow automation: an example A professional services firm managed its projects across email, spreadsheets and tools that didn't connect. A custom workflow system with automated onboarding, central project tracking and billing integration removed 15 hours of manual work a week. Client onboarding went from three days to four hours. Sometimes the right answer is the tool you can buy this afternoon. Step 4: If it's a product idea, test the riskiest assumption first Many founders end the year with a software product they still haven't built. It's tempting to start coding in December so the year counts for something. Hold off. Why most products fail CB Insights has studied startup post-mortems for years. "No market need" is consistently the most cited reason startups fail, appearing in roughly 35% to 42% of them depending on the year. Most of those products were built well. They were built for a need that wasn't there. A practical approach to MVP development Name the assumption that would sink the idea. Usually it's one of these: People will pay for this. People will change how they work to use it. We can reach buyers at a cost that makes sense. Test it as cheaply as you can. Talk to 15 to 20 likely buyers. Show them a clickable prototype. Or run the service by hand for ten customers before automating anything. Map what the product must do before anyone estimates it. We once helped define a platform from a blank page: no specification, no team, only a market thesis. The team agreed on nine areas the platform had to cover, broke each into features and functions, and fixed the scope of three delivery phases before any development began. It sounds slow, but it's what made the estimates and the progress reports mean something. Define version one by what it must prove. An MVP with one feature that proves demand beats one with twenty features that proves nothing. Decide the hard rules early. Who can see what, how money moves, what must be auditable. These are much cheaper to build in from the start than to add later. On another platform, we stored commission rates as dated records so every payout could be traced to the rate in force on the day of the sale. That removed a whole category of disputes. The last weeks of a year are a good time to validate an idea and scope phase one properly. Starting January with that done will get you to launch faster than starting December with code.
