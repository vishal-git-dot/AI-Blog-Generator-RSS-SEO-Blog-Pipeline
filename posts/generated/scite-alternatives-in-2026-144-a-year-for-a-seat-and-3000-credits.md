# Scite alternatives in 2026: $144 a year for a seat and 3,000 credits

## Overview

Citation tools are sold like journals: a seat, a year, a login. Scite's pricing page follows that convention and then adds a meter that most of its neighbours do not have — a monthly allowance of MCP credits, the units its AI assistant spends when it answers a question. On 26 November 2026 the page prints two plans and an enterprise tier. Basic is $12 a month billed yearly, with a note that yearly billing saves $96 — which is $144 a year. It includes 250 MCP credits a month, full Assistant and Search access, Smart Citation reports, collections up to 1,000 papers, and citation alerts. Pro is $40 a month billed yearly, saving $120 — $480 a year — and adds API access with $60 of API credit, 2,500 MCP credits a month, collections up to 10,000 papers, and patent, clinical-trial and grant datasets. Enterprise is custom, with pooled usage. The trial is seven days, and the page's own FAQ says the trial enrolls you in the plan you selected unless you cancel before it ends. That is worth reading twice: a "free trial" here is a subscription with a seven-day return window. Whether that is value depends on a number the page does not print: how many credits one search, one Assistant answer or one Smart Citation report consumes. Without that, the credit allowance is a ceiling, not a budget. The same ambiguity applies to the overage. Pro and Team plans can continue past the allowance on pay-as-you-go billing, charged per call on the next invoice, with a monthly spending limit set on the subscription page. The rate per call is not on the pricing page. If you plan to exceed 2,500 credits a month, ask for that rate before you choose the plan — it decides whether Pro plus overage is cheaper than Enterprise. The one place it becomes a real number is Humata's overage: $0.02 per page beyond the 500 pages included on Expert, or beyond the 5,000 on Team. A researcher reading 1,000 pages a month on Humata Expert pays $9.99 + (500 × $0.02) = $19.99 — roughly Scite Basic's yearly-billing seat, for a different job. Humata reads documents; Scite tells you how a paper was cited. They are not substitutes, which is exactly why the per-paper framing misleads. What Scite is actually for Our own catalogue entry is blunt about it: Scite indexes citation context and shows whether a paper was cited as supporting or contrasting, which gives a basis for judging reliability and speeds up the hunt for counter-evidence. The same entry warns that coverage and citation data are uneven across fields, that classification is not always right, and that Korean-language papers are less well supported. Those three sentences should decide the purchase more than the price table does. A researcher in a well-covered field gets a strong signal per credit; a researcher in a thinly covered one pays the same $144 and gets less.
