# Samsung Securities: Hackers Got Into a Website Feedback Form and Took 8,241 Customers' ID Files. Where Do the Files Your Customers Upload End Up?

## Overview

Originally published at security.hackerme.ai . Samsung Securities said hackers got into a website feedback form and took files from 8,241 customers. Where do the files your customers upload end up? On 23 November 2026, the brokerage told reporters what it had found. Unauthorized access had reached the "Voice of Customer" intake system on its website, on the afternoon of 21 November. Seoul Economic Daily and SBS, citing the company, said attackers took the files customers had attached to their inquiries. Yonhap also reported the same number of clients. What was in those files According to Seoul Economic Daily and SBS, the files covered up to 13 data fields. Those fields included names, account numbers, addresses, contact details and driver's license numbers. The fields involved vary by customer. The company said it will tell each affected customer which of their fields the attackers took. Seoul Economic Daily also said customers can check through a "Personal (Credit) Information Leak Inquiry" menu on the company's website. What the company did, as reported Samsung Securities blocked the IP address and suspended the Voice of Customer intake service, Seoul Economic Daily reported. SBS said the company also put firewall blocks in place. Both papers, and Yonhap's shorter account, said the firm notified regulators. Those bodies included the Financial Supervisory Service and the Korea Internet & Security Agency. Chief Executive Park Jong-moon issued an apology, according to Seoul Economic Daily. The company said it has found evidence of the incident only in the Voice of Customer system so far. It also said the incident does not affect customers who have never submitted a request there. It is investigating the cause and reviewing its other systems. If the company confirms actual damage, it said it will compensate in line with the law and its internal standards. Yonhap placed the incident in a wave of cyberattacks against South Korean financial firms in recent months. SBS noted a recent surge of hacking incidents in the financial sector. None of the three reports named an attacker. They also did not describe how the attackers got in, or say anyone later sold those files. So I am not going to fill those gaps. Why this matters if you run a small site Still, you do not need to be a brokerage. Almost every small-business site has a contact form, a support form, or a quote form. Many of those forms accept a file. Customers attach a copy of an ID, an invoice, a contract, or a screenshot of a problem. Those files often sit somewhere on the site, in an inbox, or in a folder the form created. If anyone can open that folder with a direct link, the file is no longer just a message to you. The same is true if too many people can open the form's admin page. I am not saying this is how the Samsung Securities incident happened. The reports do not say. I am saying this is the part a shop owner can check this week, without waiting for a full investigation. I keep thinking about the quote form on a small shop's site, because I have seen owners treat it as a mailbox. It is also a filing cabinet for other people's documents. What to check this week I would start with these checks. 1. Where the uploads are stored, and whether a direct URL or a public folder exposes them Ask your developer or host where the contact, support and quote forms save those files. Then, without circulating a customer's real file, check whether a direct link to an upload opens in a private window. Also check whether anyone can list or search the folder. If they can, treat that as urgent. I wrote earlier about a Contact Form 7 file upload flaw and about file upload security on a website . Those are different bugs and different checklists. The habit is the same: know where the file goes after someone hits send. 2. How long you keep the files, and delete the old ones Decide how long you actually need a customer's ID copy or invoice. If you have finished the job, delete the attachment. A form that keeps every file "just in case" turns into a pile of other people's documents. 3. Who can open the form's admin side List everyone who can read those submissions. That list may include staff, a freelancer, an old agency login, or a plugin support account. Remove people who no longer need access. A stronger sign-in check on those accounts is worth it. None of these checks proves you are safe. They also will not tell you whether someone already copied a file. They only shrink the pile and the number of people who can see it. Customers will still blame your name if someone takes their file, even when a vendor or a form tool sits in the middle. I wrote about that after a store app that could see customer addresses and after a payment vendor that held card data .
