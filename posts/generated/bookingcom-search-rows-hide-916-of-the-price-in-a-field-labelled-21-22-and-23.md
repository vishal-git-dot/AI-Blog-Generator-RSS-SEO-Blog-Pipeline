# Booking.com Search Rows Hide 9–16% of the Price in a Field Labelled 21, 22 and 23

## Overview

I run a visa agency in Bali. The other half of my week goes into scrapers on Apify, and one of them reads Booking.com search pages: destination in, one row per property out, with the price for your dates, the matched room, the rating and the location. The field people build their spreadsheet on is priceTotal . It is the number Booking prints in the search card, it is the number the site sorts by, and it is not the number anybody pays. On 12 October 2026 I ran the same stay — 20 to 23 November 2026, two adults, one room — against four destinations and read all 24 rows. Booking's GraphQL search response returns excluded charges as a list of objects carrying chargeMode , chargeInclusion , chargeType and amountPerStay — and for an anonymous caller chargeType is an integer. There is no translation node next to it, the way there is on badges, cancellation copy and review text. The site renders "+ taxes and charges" and hides the breakdown behind a tooltip that is assembled elsewhere. My first version of this mapper expected names and produced "CITYTAX: US$18.24" in a code comment that never matched reality. The field was empty on every row for a while, because I was reading a translation key that is not there. Across the four markets the codes behaved consistently enough to describe, though not enough to label: 22 was always a flat amount per stay, identical across every property in a city: €24 in Lisbon, $10.50 in New York, $0 in Tokyo and on three Bali rows. 21 and 23 were always a percentage of priceTotal : 6% in Lisbon, 14.75% in New York, 10% in Tokyo, 9.01% under code 21 and 10.99% under code 23 in Bali. I ship the integers. I am not going to guess that 22 means "city tax" and put a word in a dataset column on the strength of six rows per market, because the moment I do, someone builds a VAT reclaim report on it. An unexplained 22 makes you go look. A wrong CITYTAX does not. What it does to a sorted table The percentage is near-constant inside a city, so within-city ranking mostly survives. Which brings me to the bug this article found in my own code. The zero that looked like a missing value West Side YMCA, cheapest row in the New York sort at $370.15, came back like this: { "taxesAndFeesExcluded" : [ "23: $0" ], "taxesAndFeesExcludedTotal" : null } ["23: $0"] is real: Booking returns the charge line with an amount of zero. But my aggregate was null , because the mapper ended in || null — and in JavaScript 0 || null is null . A property with genuinely no extra charges was indistinguishable from a property whose charges I failed to read. On a 25-row New York search, two rows were affected: West Side YMCA and HI New York City Hostel, both hostel-style properties. If you pulled this Actor's data before 12 October 2026 and treated taxesAndFeesExcludedTotal: null as "unknown, skip the row", you dropped the cheapest properties in the market. The other half of the row is prose, not data While confirming the tax numbers I changed the language input and watched the same property come back four ways. MASA Hotel & Spa, Lisbon, same dates: language distanceFromCentre en-us (default) 2.6 miles from downtown en-gb 2.6 miles from centre de Zentrum: 4,2 km fr 4,2 km du centre Three things worth knowing before you parse that column: It is a sentence, not a number. There is no numeric distance field in the search response — Booking sends location.mainDistance already rendered. English means imperial, even British English. en-gb changed "downtown" to "centre" and left the miles alone. Metric arrives only when you leave English, and then with a comma decimal separator: 4,2 km . The wording changes, so your regex is locale-bound. de puts the label first and a colon in the middle. Use latitude and longitude instead and compute the distance yourself. They are plain floats and they do not translate. The same split hits reviewScoreWord : Wonderful in English, Sehr gut in German, Très bien in French. Filter on that string and your pipeline silently empties the day someone sets a language. Filter on reviewScore , which stays 9.3 . What does not translate is the half of the row my own code assembles: propertyType and roomBeds stayed Hotel and 1 king bed under de and fr , because those come from integer→name tables inside the Actor, not from Booking. So a non-English run gives you a row that is half localised and half English, and the seam runs exactly along "who mapped this field". Worth knowing if you ever diff two runs and wonder why only some columns moved. One more locale trap: priceTotalText is a formatted string — "$147.25" but "€ 524.51" , with a non-breaking space and the symbol on the other side. Parse priceTotal , which is a float. Region searches have no centre Bali returned destinationType: "REGION" and distanceFromCentre: null on all six rows. Lisbon, New York and Tokyo came back as "CITY" and filled it on every row. That is not a failure — a 5,780 km² island does not have a downtown — but if your dashboard has a "distance from centre" axis, it will be empty for every region, landmark and country-level search you run. Check destinationType before you plot it. When this is the wrong tool You need to actually book. This reads public search data. Taking a reservation means Booking's affiliate programme, which is a contract, not a scraper. You need yesterday's prices. Nothing stores the past. Hotel pricing is only observable going forward, so schedule a daily run now if you want a history in March. You need the exact final figure at checkout. The excluded charges are Booking's own estimate of what the property will add; resort fees, card fees and local levies collected at the desk are not all in there. You need every rate of 500 hotels. Rooms and rates mean a request per property. That is fine for fifty and slow for five hundred.
