# # Job Offer & Task Scams: How to Spot a Fake Recruiter Before You Pay

## Overview

Published: August 1, 2026 · 11 min read Job scams have moved off obviously sketchy classifieds sites and onto LinkedIn, WhatsApp, and Telegram — platforms people already trust with their real job search. The pitch varies — a remote role, a "task" that pays per completion, an app-testing gig — but the goal is almost always the same: get money or personal data from you before any real employer enters the picture. This article covers how these scams actually run, the red flags in the offer itself and in onboarding, how to verify a recruiter or company in a few minutes, and what to do if you've already paid or shared information. Hard stops: if you're asked to pay for training or equipment, deposit a check and send part of it elsewhere, or receive and reship packages — stop. These disqualify the offer regardless of how legitimate everything else looks. You're recruited to "optimise" listings or leave reviews for an app or online store, earning a small commission per task. Early tasks pay out instantly to build trust. Eventually a task requires you to deposit your own money to "unlock" a higher commission tier — and the balance, along with your deposit, becomes permanently unwithdrawable. Fake recruiter outreach. A message arrives out of nowhere — often on LinkedIn, then quickly moved to WhatsApp or Telegram — offering a well-paid remote role with minimal qualifications required. The recruiter's profile photo, company name, and even a fake careers page are often real details stolen or spoofed to look legitimate. Overpayment / check cashing scams. You're "hired", sent a check for equipment or supplies that's larger than needed, and told to deposit it and wire back the difference. The check later bounces — days after you've already wired real money from your own account. Reshipping and "money mule" jobs. A "logistics coordinator" or "quality control" role asks you to receive packages at your home address and reship them elsewhere, or to move money through your personal bank account. Both are typically laundering stolen goods or fraud proceeds — and you can be held legally liable even if you didn't know. Pay-to-start onboarding. After a fast, informal "interview" — sometimes text-only — you're told the role requires you to purchase your own equipment, software licences, or a "training kit" through a specific link, with a promise of reimbursement in your first paycheck that never arrives. Red Flags in the Job Offer Itself Pay that's high relative to the effort described. A vague role description paired with an unusually high hourly rate for minimal experience is the single most common tell. Contact moves off the hiring platform immediately. A recruiter who messages you on LinkedIn and asks to continue on WhatsApp or Telegram within the first message is avoiding a platform with reporting tools and a paper trail. You're hired with no real interview. A same-day hire based on a short chat, with no call and no discussion of your actual experience, is a scripted process designed to move fast before you think it through. Urgency to accept or start immediately. Pressure to respond within hours is designed to short-circuit the research you'd otherwise do. The job description is vague or generic. Postings that describe pay and flexibility in detail but say almost nothing concrete about daily responsibilities are frequently templated across dozens of fake listings. Red Flags During the Interview or Onboarding Requests for sensitive data before an official offer letter. A legitimate employer collects your SSN, bank details, or ID only after a formal, signed offer — not informally in chat. You're told to buy your own equipment through a specific link. Real employers provide equipment directly or reimburse through a normal expense process — they don't require one specific vendor link with promised reimbursement "in your first check". You're asked to deposit a check and send part of it elsewhere. No legitimate onboarding ever requires this. It's check fraud, and you're liable once the check bounces. Communication comes from a personal email domain. An "HR" contact emailing from Gmail, or a domain that only loosely resembles the real company's, is one of the easiest scams to catch — compare it character-by-character against the company's actual website. The recruiter's photo doesn't match who they claim to be. A reverse face search on the profile photo can reveal if it belongs to someone else entirely, or appears across multiple unrelated "recruiter" profiles. How to Verify Before You Pay or Share Anything Go to the company's official careers page directly. Type the URL yourself and check whether the role is actually listed there. Search the recruiter's name and company together. A search for "[recruiter name] scam" or "[company name] hiring scam" surfaces reports from prior targets remarkably often. Call the company through a number you find independently. Not one given to you in the offer — this single step defeats the majority of fake-recruiter scripts. Never pay to get a job. Legitimate employment never requires payment for training, equipment, or "processing fees" before you've received a paycheck. Don't deposit checks or move money on someone else's instruction. If onboarding involves depositing a check and forwarding money, or reshipping packages, stop immediately. What to Do If You've Already Paid or Shared Information Contact your bank or payment provider immediately. Some transfers can still be reversed or flagged within a narrow window, and your bank needs to know a fraudulent check was deposited to your account. Change passwords and freeze your credit. If you shared your SSN, ID, or bank details, place a fraud alert or credit freeze with the major credit bureaus. Report it to the platform where contact started. This gets the listing taken down and can prevent the same script from reaching other people. File a report with the FTC and IC3. Reportfraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov) both take job scam reports. Stop any reshipping or fund transfers immediately. Keep records of everything — dates, tracking numbers, instructions received — in case you need to demonstrate you were an unknowing participant.
