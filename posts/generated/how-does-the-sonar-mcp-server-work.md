# How does the SONAR MCP server work?

How does the SONAR MCP server work? The SONAR MCP server (SONAR, the freight market intelligence platform covered by FreightWaves; rollout to SONAR customers began the week of November 9, 2026, launch article published November 10, 2026) puts SONAR's freight data inside AI assistants such as Claude and Copilot: the assistant decides which SONAR tools to call based on the question asked and the user's SONAR permissions, then combines the results into a plain-language answer. Counted from SONAR's own MCP overview document and the November 10, 2026 FreightWaves launch article, both read first-hand today: 24 tools, every one of them read-only — the overview states verbatim that "the assistant selects the relevant read-only MCP tools from your permitted SONAR capabilities" — across 6 tool groups (Rates and Projections 7, Market Conditions 4, SONAR Index Data 4, Lane Intelligence 3, FMCSA Carrier Safety 3, Ocean and Utilities 3) and 4 freight modes (truckload, intermodal, LTL, ocean). In SONAR's own F3 demo, the assistant compared truckload and intermodal rates for a shipper moving 500 truckloads a year from Los Angeles to Chicago, answered that switching to intermodal would save money, flagged Chicago to Louisville as the most exposed lane in a holiday scenario, and suggested locking in contract rates there before the holiday season. That is a procurement recommendation — a suggestion to commit a freight budget — produced with 0 per-action decision scores, 0 confidence thresholds for acting, 0 jurors, and 0 sealed decision receipts named anywhere in the launch surfaces. (SONAR does publish one score: a 0–100 lane ease-of-coverage index. That is a market-data index, not a score of whether an act should happen.) The tools being read-only is a real safety property — the MCP server itself cannot lock a rate or spend a dollar. The risk lives one step later, where the recommendation meets a person or a downstream system that acts on it, unscored. That deciding layer is what ScriptMasterLabs sells. But SONAR's own marketing is built around decisions — its overview is titled "Built for the decisions freight teams make," and its demo's payoff moment is a recommendation to lock in contract rates. A recommendation is where the money decision actually happens in most freight teams: the human who hears "lock this lane before the holidays" from an assistant that just pulled four data sources is ratifying, not deciding. Nothing in the chain scores that recommendation, records why it was made, or seals a refusal when the answer should have been no. The decision-gated question is the one the launch never asks: before a rate gets locked, a mode gets switched, or a budget gets committed on an agent's say-so — what scored whether it should happen at all?
