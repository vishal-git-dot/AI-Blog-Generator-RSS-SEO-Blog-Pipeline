# The clause on page four: what a POS contract does to a restaurant's menu page

## Overview

Ask a restaurant owner what their website costs and you get one number: "$89 a month, and it includes the till." One bill, so it reads as one product. It is not. It is a term contract for the restaurant's operating system with a price list page stapled to it — and the page is the cheapest thing in the bundle, which is exactly why it is the thing that gets bundled. That distinction only matters at one moment: when you want out. The page can be replaced in an afternoon. The contract is the part with a signature, a term, and an exit fee printed somewhere around page four. I read the actual agreements rather than the pricing pages (both, on 14 November 2026) while writing the long version of this for our site. Here is what is in them, what the exit fee arithmetic looks like with real numbers, and the structure that keeps a restaurant's menu page outside the contract entirely. What the three contracts actually say Vendor The term Leaving early Toast — Merchant Agreement §8.1, §8.4 (updated 10 Sep 2025) Initial term is whatever the order form sets; after that it renews for successive one-year periods, and either side can stop it with 30 days' written notice before the term ends. An early termination fee equal to the remaining software subscription fees for the rest of the term — or, on a pay-as-you-go subscription, $150 for each month remaining — plus any processing fee tied to software financing. Toast — support.toasttab.com, POS contract and renewal terms (updated 26 Oct 2026) In their own words: "Toast does not offer a month-to-month option after the initial term." Same clause; the support article just makes the absence of a monthly exit explicit. Clover — clover.com/pricing FAQ The subscription term; at the end of it you either purchase or return the equipment, and you must tell Clover at least 30 days before the end if you are not continuing, or the term extends at the same monthly charge. "Subscriptions are non-cancelable and ineligible for refunds." Square — squareup.com/us/en/pricing Published plans are monthly: Free $0, Plus $49, Premium $149 per location. "No hidden fees or locked-in contracts. Cancel or switch anytime." Three things follow, and none of them is "POS companies are bad". 1. Lock-in is not a rumour, it is a clause — and it varies. It is in §8.4, in a pricing FAQ, or not there at all, and it differs by vendor and by the order form you signed. The pricing page will not tell you. The document will. 2. What is financed is hardware and software, not food and not your menu text. A page with twelve prices on it is not part of that bargain in any technical sense, even when it arrives on the same invoice and behind the same login. 3. The exit fee is proportional to time, not to use. "$150 for each month remaining" is a number you can compute before signing: Months left on the term Exit fee at $150/month 6 $900 12 $1,800 24 $3,600 36 $5,400 Two years left on a pay-as-you-go plan is $3,600, printed in the agreement, payable whether or not the restaurant opens on Mondays. If your order form sets a subscription term instead, the clause is the remaining subscription fees: 18 months at $89 is $1,602. This is not an argument that a POS is overpriced. A till that runs cards, prints to the kitchen, decrements stock and reports on covers is doing real work, and $49–$149 a month for that work can be cheap. The claim is narrower: the menu page is not that work , and buying it inside a contract with a term and an exit fee is how a shop ends up renting a page it could have owned. The two questions to ask in writing this week If someone is selling you a POS, the pricing page is not the document that binds you. Ask, in an email, before the install date: How many months is the initial term? (The agreement only says the order form sets it. The order form is the answer.) What is the early termination fee in dollars, for that term? Not "pro-rated" — the figure. A salesperson who will not put both in an email is telling you something. And if the answer includes "the website is free with the package", ask the third question: what happens to the page, the address and the QR codes if I cancel at month fourteen? That third question is the one worth designing around, because the failure mode is not money. It is a stack of printed table tents, a door poster and a Google listing all pointing at a page that stops answering when the till contract ends. Printed paper cannot be edited. Strip the page back to what it actually does A restaurant's page has five jobs, and not one of them involves a till: Say what things cost — the ten or twelve things a guest asks about, at the price you charge at the table. Say when you are open — including the closed day the internet always gets wrong. Say where you are — a Directions button that opens the guest's own maps app. Let them call — a number that dials when tapped, because half of them will. Be the one address that a QR code and a Google listing point at — and never change it. Every one of those is static text. Static text is the cheapest thing to serve on the internet, which is what makes the monthly billing strange rather than the one-off price. Google's own rules make the page matter more, not less. The Business Profile has a menu editor for food and drink businesses; the Help page says changes can take 24–48 hours to appear, and that Google may transcribe menu data from your business website with an opt-out. Read that as an owner and the priority is obvious: if Google can take the menu from your site, the site is the version of the truth and the POS is a copy of it that also takes orders. The structure that stays outside the contract This is the part that is actually engineering, and it is small: 1. A page you hold the edit link to. Static HTML behind a URL that never changes: /s/your-shop . Nobody's subscription gates the prices; you change a line at 6pm from a phone and the page changes in a minute. Ours is $0 on a free plan (there is a paid tier for a photo gallery and a custom domain, $6.99 and $13.99 if you want them — but the $0 page is not a trial). 2. Printed pieces that encode the address, not the price. This is the decision that makes the whole thing work: a static QR code encodes the URL. Because the URL never changes, the code never needs re-issuing — not after a price change, not after a menu rewrite, not after a POS migration. The subscription version of QR ("dynamic QR", typically $20–30/month) exists to let a printed short link be re-pointed later, which is the problem you have only if your address moves. Print once, at the sizes QR codes need (our measured A4 poster code is 126 mm; the table tent's is 62 mm; more on the millimetre maths here ). 3. One chain, three places. The page goes into the website field on the Business Profile, the QR goes on the door, the same address goes in the Instagram bio. Break it anywhere and the guest lands on something that is not a menu. 4. Keep the POS for the till. Nothing above asks it to change. Orders still arrive where they arrived; the difference is that the prices the internet sees are yours to edit without a support ticket. What $49 buys, and what it does not The kit is fifteen files for a shop with tables, eight for one without: the live page, an A4 QR poster, the standalone QR PNG, a print menu, numbered table cards, table tents, 70 mm stickers, Wi-Fi cards, a menu-screen file, an edit runbook, delivery-app closure notices, review cards, a Google Business Profile sheet and a delivery note. What it does not do: no cart, no card payments on the shop's page, no table ordering, no reservations, no stock. If a restaurant needs those, that is a POS decision with a POS price — and pretending otherwise would make a worse product than saying so. The whole argument fits in one sentence: buy the till for the till, and keep the thing your customer reads before they arrive outside the contract, so that the paper on the door keeps working after any supplier you leave.
