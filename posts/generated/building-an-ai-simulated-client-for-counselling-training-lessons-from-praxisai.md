# Building an AI-Simulated Client for Counselling Training: Lessons From PraxisAI

## Overview

An AI-simulated client is an AI-powered character that allows trainees to practise realistic counselling conversations without requiring a human role-play partner for every session. The system can respond to what the trainee says, maintain a scenario, adapt the conversation and provide structured feedback after the session. That is the idea behind PraxisAI , an AI human-skills simulation and assessment platform built by ThinkShift. Trainee counsellors can practise conversations with AI clients through voice, avatar and chat, with sessions evaluated against defined competencies. The interesting engineering challenge isn't simply making an AI that can talk. It is making an AI that can stay inside a scenario, behave consistently, create a useful training experience and turn a conversation into measurable feedback. That became the core problem we explored while building PraxisAI. What Is an AI-Simulated Client? An AI-simulated client is a virtual client powered by AI for training or role-play. Instead of following a fixed dialogue tree, the simulated client can respond dynamically to the trainee's questions and statements. This makes the interaction less predictable than traditional scripted practice. For counselling training, that distinction matters. A trainee shouldn't only practise asking a predefined list of questions. They need to experience what happens when a conversation changes direction, when a client gives an unexpected answer, or when important information emerges gradually. Recent research has started evaluating generative AI in exactly this role. Studies have found that AI can provide a flexible environment for practising counselling skills, while also identifying limitations around emotional nuance, authenticity, cultural dynamics and overly agreeable responses. So the product challenge becomes: How do you make an AI conversation useful for learning, rather than simply interesting to interact with? Why Use AI for Counselling Role-Play? Traditional role-play is valuable, but it has practical limitations. Two students need to be available at the same time. One person has to play the client. The scenario may change depending on the person playing the role. And repeating the same exercise many times isn't always easy. An AI client introduces another option. A trainee can practise a scenario, review the interaction, try again and compare the results. That creates a learning loop: Practice → Conversation → Feedback → Reflection → Practice again The value is not that AI replaces a supervisor or a human client. The value is that it can create additional opportunities to practise between structured teaching and real-world experience. Research published in 2025 similarly found that AI-simulated counselling sessions could offer a psychologically safer and flexible environment for practice, while concluding that AI should supplement rather than replace live training. The Real Challenge: Making the Client Feel Consistent A generic chatbot can answer questions. A useful training simulator needs to do more. Imagine a scenario where a client is initially reluctant to discuss a particular problem. If the trainee asks an open-ended question, the client might gradually reveal more. If the trainee interrupts or asks a leading question, the client might respond differently. If the trainee demonstrates empathy and creates space, the conversation may develop in another direction. The system therefore needs to maintain more than conversational fluency. It needs to maintain scenario state . That means keeping track of things such as: the client's background the scenario and presenting concern information already disclosed information that has not yet been disclosed the client's conversational behaviour the training objective the counselling approach being practised the progression of the conversation Without this context, an AI client can quickly start behaving like a generic assistant. And that breaks the simulation. Designing Realistic AI Role-Play Realism doesn't mean making the AI sound human in every sentence. In a training environment, realism means creating behaviour that makes sense within the scenario . A simulated client may: hesitate before answering avoid a subject provide incomplete information change their level of openness ask the trainee a question react differently to different counselling approaches reveal information progressively misunderstand something become more comfortable as the conversation develops These behaviours create opportunities for the trainee to practise actual communication skills. Research into AI-simulated counselling has identified a similar challenge: AI can produce convincing interactions, but overly idealised responses, limited emotional nuance and missing non-verbal cues can reduce authenticity. That means realism has to be treated as a product design problem , not just an LLM prompt. From AI Conversation to Training Simulation This was one of the biggest distinctions in building PraxisAI. A chatbot answers a user's message. A training simulator has a learning objective . Those are very different systems. For example, the objective might be to help a trainee practise reflective listening. The system then needs to evaluate the conversation against that competency rather than simply deciding whether the final answer sounded good. This creates a second layer around the AI conversation: AI client → conversation → competency evaluation → feedback That second layer is where the product becomes a training system. Why Feedback Matters A realistic conversation is useful. A realistic conversation followed by useful feedback is much more useful. After a practice session, trainees need to understand what happened. Instead of receiving a generic score such as: "Good job — 8/10." the feedback should connect the assessment to the actual interaction. For example: Where did the trainee demonstrate a specific competency? Which response could have been handled differently? Did the trainee ask too many questions? Did they reflect what the client was saying? Did they move too quickly through an important disclosure? What could they try differently in the next session? This creates a connection between what happened and what to practise next . PraxisAI is designed around this assessment layer, with sessions evaluated against competencies rather than treating the conversation as an isolated chatbot interaction. Designing Competency-Based Assessment One of the interesting product challenges is that counselling isn't one single skill. Different approaches can emphasise different techniques and competencies. So an assessment system needs to understand the context in which a conversation is taking place. That means the evaluation layer needs to consider: Scenario + approach + conversation + competency framework rather than: Conversation + generic AI score This is important because the same response could be appropriate in one context and less useful in another. The assessment therefore becomes part of the product architecture, not something added after the AI conversation has been built. Practice and Assessment Are Different Modes Another useful distinction is between practice and assessment . During practice, the trainee is learning. They may want guidance, feedback and opportunities to repeat a scenario. During assessment, the objective is different: the trainee should demonstrate their skills with less intervention. Separating these experiences makes the product more useful because the system is supporting two different user goals. This is a broader lesson for AI product development: Don't build one AI experience and expect it to serve every stage of the user's journey. The AI behaviour, interface and evaluation system should change according to what the user is trying to accomplish. Building for Voice, Avatar and Chat PraxisAI is designed around multiple interaction formats, including voice, avatar and chat. This creates another product consideration. Text-based interaction gives the system a clean representation of the conversation. Voice introduces additional dimensions such as: pacing pauses tone speaking behaviour turn-taking An avatar adds another visual layer to the experience. The underlying training objective, however, should remain consistent. The interface can change without losing the core learning loop: Interact → Evaluate → Understand → Improve That separation between the interaction layer and the learning layer is useful when designing AI training products. What We Learned About AI Simulation 1. A prompt is not a product It is easy to demonstrate an AI role-play by writing a detailed prompt. Building a reliable product around that interaction is much harder. You need scenario management, user flows, evaluation logic, feedback, persistence and a clear learning objective. 2. Realism needs boundaries Giving an AI complete freedom doesn't necessarily produce the best simulation. Training scenarios need constraints. The AI should have enough freedom to create natural conversations while remaining consistent with the scenario and learning objective. 3. Evaluation needs structure If every assessment is generated from scratch, results can become inconsistent. A useful training system needs defined competencies and a consistent evaluation framework. The AI can then interpret the conversation within those boundaries. 4. The conversation is only one part of the experience The most visible part of an AI simulator is the conversation. But the less visible parts, scenario setup, evaluation, feedback and progress tracking, may create much of the product's actual value. 5. AI should support human learning This is perhaps the most important lesson. AI simulation can create more practice opportunities, but it doesn't reproduce everything that happens in a real human interaction. Current research continues to identify limitations around emotional depth, authenticity, cultural sensitivity and non-verbal communication. So the right goal isn't: "Replace human counselling practice with AI." It's: "Use AI to make structured practice more accessible and repeatable." Building an AI Product in Dubai PraxisAI is one example of the type of AI product development we work on at ThinkShift . ThinkShift is based in Dubai and focuses on building working products that can be tested with real users rather than spending months building around assumptions. That approach influenced how we thought about PraxisAI. Instead of asking: "How can we build a chatbot for counsellors?" the more useful question was: "What would make AI genuinely useful for someone learning counselling skills?" That change in framing affected the product. The AI conversation became one component. The scenario became another. The competency framework became another. And the feedback loop connected everything together. That is an important distinction when building AI products in Dubai—or anywhere else. The technology is only one part of the product. The real work is connecting the technology to a measurable user problem. Can AI Replace Human Role-Play? No. AI-simulated clients should be considered a supplement to human-led training , not a replacement for supervisors, educators or real human interaction. Research from 2025 found promising results for AI-assisted counselling practice, including improvements in trainee confidence and reductions in anxiety in one study, but the researchers also emphasised the need for thoughtful integration and further research. Human interaction still brings qualities that AI cannot perfectly reproduce: emotional complexity non-verbal communication cultural context professional judgement unpredictable behaviour genuine interpersonal relationships AI can provide another layer of practice.
