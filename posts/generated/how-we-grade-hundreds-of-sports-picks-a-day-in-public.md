# How we grade hundreds of sports picks a day in public

## Overview

Most sports prediction sites will tell you how good they are. Very few will show you the losses. We decided early that our record would be a public page rather than a marketing claim, and that decision ended up shaping the whole pipeline. This is a write-up of how the grading works, what it costs, and what we learned when we pointed the same machinery at the betting market itself. The shape of the pipeline There are four stages, and each one is a table you can query. 1. Publish before the game. A pick only counts if it exists before the event starts. Models run on a schedule, write their selections to a table, and every row is stamped with the time it was written and the price that was on screen at that moment. Nothing is allowed to be added later. This is the part everyone skips, and it is the only part that matters: a record that can be edited after the result is not a record. 2. Wait for the official result. A separate job pulls final scores. Scores arrive messy: a game is postponed, a player withdraws, a line is voided, two feeds disagree on whether the match went to extra time. Every one of those cases needs an explicit rule written down in advance, not a judgement call made when you can already see whether the pick was going to win. 3. Grade. Join picks to final scores, apply the market rule (did the total go over, did the side cover, did the favourite win), and write win, loss or void. A pick graded once is frozen. We do not regrade history when a model changes; the old era's record stays as it was, closed and dated. 4. Show it. The graded rows go straight onto a public page, newest first, with the price we published, the result, and what $100 would have done. Every dip stays in the chart. The non-obvious engineering lesson: stage 1 and stage 3 must not be able to touch each other. Our worst bug in this system was not a modelling error. It was a grading job and a settlement job running at the same time on the same rows, so a read during a delete saw a half-finished world. Grading is a lock-ordering problem long before it is a statistics problem. Why publishing the losses is the whole point Three reasons, in increasing order of how much they surprised us. The obvious one: it is honest, and it is the only defence against the survivorship problem that makes every tipster's record look good. If you can delete a pick, your record means nothing, and nobody can tell from the outside whether you did. The second one: it makes you define things. You cannot publish a result without deciding, in public and in advance, what a push is, what a void is, which price counts, and what happens when a game is abandoned. Half the value of a public record is the rulebook it forces you to write. The third one, which we did not expect: it changes what you build. When every loss is visible, a model that wins slightly more often but explodes in a bad week looks worse on the page than it does in a backtest summary. The public chart, with the drawdowns left in, is a better product decision-making tool than any single aggregate number. Our live record, as published at the time of writing, covers 242 picks since the current selling rules were rebuilt on 5 September 2026, and the era before it graded 3,222 picks: 2,440 won, 782 lost. Both eras are on the page together, the retired one frozen. What happened when we graded the market instead of ourselves Once you have a grading archive, you can point it at a much more interesting question: is the betting market itself a good forecaster? We took every settled two-outcome market in the archive, 18,238 of them across five sports from November 2024 to July 2026, stripped the bookmaker's margin out of each price to turn it into a probability, and checked how often those things actually happened. Both sides of every market are included, so the dataset nets to 50/50 by construction and contains no information about anyone's selections. The answer is uncomfortable if you sell predictions: the market is very well calibrated. Across every probability band, real outcome rates land within about two percentage points of the margin-free price. Something priced as a 65% chance happened about 63% of the time; a 25% chance happened about 27% of the time. Margin-free probability band Observations Mean implied Actually happened Gap 20–30% 5,263 25.2% 26.7% +1.4 40–50% 5,515 45.7% 46.4% +0.7 60–70% 4,945 65.2% 63.0% −2.2 80–90% 2,157 82.5% 80.9% −1.6 There is a systematic residual, and it points the same way every time: outcomes priced below 50% happen slightly more often than their margin-free price implies, and favourites slightly less. That is the classic favourite–longshot pattern, documented since the 1940s, measured here on modern multi-sport fixed-odds markets with the margin removed. It is roughly twice as large in player-prop markets as in match-level markets, and those are the same markets that carry the fattest margins. So the honest framing is not "we beat the market". It is: the market is nearly perfectly calibrated, the cost of a bet is real and measurable, and the only way anyone should judge a prediction service is a record that includes the bad weeks.
