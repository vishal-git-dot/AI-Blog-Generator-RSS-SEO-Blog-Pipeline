# Exemplary AI alternatives in 2026: $19 buys 6.7 hours of transcription, and the hour is the real unit

## Overview

Most AI transcription tools sell a seat and describe what the plan can do. Exemplary AI sells a seat and tells you how many hours of audio come with it — and, unusually, prints the conversion rate between credits and minutes. That one line changes the comparison. On 26 November 2026 the pricing page states the rule at the bottom: 1 minute transcribed costs 5 credits; 1 minute of new video turned into shorts costs 15 credits. So the same 60 minutes of audio is 300 credits as a transcript or 900 credits as a set of clips, and the plan you need depends entirely on which of those two jobs you are buying. Moving from Creator to Business multiplies the price by 7.8 and the included hours by 10 — the top plan is a volume discount, not a different product. The shorts column is where budgets break. An hour of finished short-form video costs three times an hour of transcription in credits, because 15 credits a minute is three times 5. A podcaster who transcribes four one-hour episodes a month needs 1,200 credits; the same four episodes also cut into shorts need 3,600. On Creator's 2,000 credits, the first fits and the second does not. What the free plan actually covers 300 credits is one hour of transcription, once. Not one hour a month — the card says "300 credits, once", and the paid plans are the ones described as monthly. Put a normal calendar against that. One weekly one-hour meeting is about 4.3 hours a month, or 1,290 credits: four times the free allowance, and inside Creator's 6.7 hours. Two such meetings, plus a monthly webinar, is about 10 hours, which needs Pro. A team that records all-hands and client calls will pass 20 hours without trying, which is where Business starts. The other free limits matter more than the credits for some users: 720p exports carry a watermark, videos are capped at 30 minutes, uploads at 500 MB, and the 20 minutes of shorts are gone after the first clip batch. This is the practical problem with "cheapest alternative" lists for audio tools: a $6 seat and a $19 seat are not prices for the same thing. ElevenLabs sells synthesis, Otter sells meeting notes, Suno and Udio sell music, and Exemplary sells transcription, translation and clips from one credit pool. If you only need one of those jobs, the specialist at $6–$17 is almost certainly cheaper per unit. If you need two, the arithmetic flips: a $12 caption tool and an $18 meeting recorder cost $30, against Exemplary's $19 Creator seat.
