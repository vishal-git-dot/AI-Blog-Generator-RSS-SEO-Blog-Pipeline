# How to Remove Hum and Buzz From Audio (50Hz vs 60Hz Explained)

## Overview

Originally published at https://noisewipe.com/blog/remove-hum-and-buzz-from-audio You finish a recording, play it back, and there it is: a low, steady drone sitting under every word. Sometimes it is a soft hum you only notice in the pauses. Sometimes it is a harsh, nasal buzz that makes the whole take sound cheap. Both usually come from the same place: the electricity running through the walls, the cables, and the gear around your microphone. The good news is that this kind of noise is very predictable, which makes it one of the easier problems to fix, both while recording and after the fact. This guide explains where hum and buzz come from, why the 50Hz vs 60Hz difference matters, how to stop it at the source, and how to remove hum from audio online when the recording is already done. It also covers what cleanup can't fix, so you know what to expect. What Hum and Buzz Actually Are Mains electricity is alternating current. It changes direction many times per second, and that rate is called the mains frequency (or utility frequency). When some of that electrical energy leaks into your audio path, you hear it as a tone at that frequency. 50Hz vs 60Hz: it depends on where you record There are two mains frequencies in use around the world: 60Hz in the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, and much of the rest of the Americas, plus a few places in Asia such as South Korea and Saudi Arabia. 50Hz across most of the rest of the world, including Europe, the UK, China, India, Australia, and Africa, and some South American countries such as Argentina and Chile. Japan uses both. Eastern Japan, including Tokyo, runs at 50Hz, while western Japan, including Osaka and Nagoya, runs at 60Hz. So a recording made in London hums at a slightly lower pitch than one made in New York. That matters when you fix it by hand, because you need to target the right frequency. Hum vs buzz Hum is a low, smooth drone at the mains frequency itself, 50Hz or 60Hz. It is often caused by a ground loop. Buzz is harsher and more nasal. It usually sits at double the mains frequency (100Hz or 120Hz) with more tones stacked above it, called harmonics. It typically comes from power supplies or light dimmers. In practice, real recordings often contain the base tone plus a whole series of harmonics, which is why a single cut at 50Hz or 60Hz rarely makes the noise disappear. Common Causes of Hum and Buzz Ground loops. When two pieces of connected gear, such as a computer and an audio interface or mixer, are plugged into different outlets, small voltage differences between their grounds can push current through the audio cable's shield. That current becomes hum. Unbalanced cables. Guitar cables and RCA leads have no built-in noise rejection, so they pick up interference, especially over long runs or when laid next to power cords. Laptop chargers. Many people hear hum appear the moment they plug a laptop in and disappear when it runs on battery. Some chargers also add their own whine. Dimmer switches. Traditional dimmers chop up the mains waveform to lower the brightness, and that chopping radiates buzz into nearby cables. If the buzz stops when the lights go off, you have found the culprit. Air conditioners, fridges, and fans. These add a mix of mechanical rumble and electrical hum from their motors, which is why AC noise in a video often sounds like a hum and a hiss at the same time. Fluorescent lights and cheap power strips. Both are common sources of buzz in home studios and offices. How to Stop Hum While Recording Fixing hum at the source always beats fixing it later. Before your next session, try these: Unplug one thing at a time. Record a few seconds of silence, then disconnect devices one by one until the hum stops. That tells you which connection to fix. Run the laptop on battery. If the hum disappears when the charger is out, record on battery or plug everything into the same outlet or power strip. Use balanced cables. XLR and TRS balanced connections are designed to cancel interference picked up along the cable. Keep cable runs short where you can. Keep audio cables away from power cables. If they have to cross, cross them at a right angle instead of running them side by side. Turn off dimmers and fluorescent lights in the room, or switch them to full brightness, while you record. Use a ground lift or isolation transformer on a DI box or interface if your gear has one. Never remove the ground pin from a power plug. It is unsafe, and audio equipment manufacturers warn against it. Switch off the AC for the few minutes you need a clean take. How to Remove Hum From a Finished Recording If the recording is already done, there are two main approaches. Option 1: Notch filters and EQ Because hum sits at known frequencies, you can cut those frequencies with very narrow notch filters. In Audacity, for example, the Notch Filter effect lets you set a frequency and a width. Find out whether your hum is at 50Hz or 60Hz. A spectrum view in your editor will show a tall peak. Apply a narrow notch at that frequency. Repeat for the harmonics: 100Hz, 150Hz, 200Hz for 50Hz hum, or 120Hz, 180Hz, 240Hz for 60Hz hum. Add a gentle high-pass filter below the lowest part of the voice if rumble remains. This works well when the hum is a clean, steady tone. The downsides are that it takes time, every notch also removes a slice of the voice, and it does nothing for hiss, fans, or other broadband noise mixed in with the hum. Many editors also offer dedicated hum removal plugins that automate the harmonic notches, which is a good choice if you already work in one.
