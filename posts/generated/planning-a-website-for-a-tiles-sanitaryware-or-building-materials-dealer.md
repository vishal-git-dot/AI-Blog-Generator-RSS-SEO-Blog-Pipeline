# Planning a website for a tiles, sanitaryware or building materials dealer

## Overview

A dealer’s website should help a customer answer three practical questions before making a trip: do you handle the kind of material I need, what information should I bring, and how do I reach the right person? For tiles, sanitaryware and building materials, a clear catalogue and contact path are often more useful than a complicated storefront. Start with the questions customers ask Write down the questions staff hear in the showroom or over the phone. They might ask about tile size, finish, colour variation, bathroom fittings, cement or other product categories, delivery coverage, or what to measure before visiting. Use only categories that match your actual stock and service. The list becomes a simple plan for the site: category pages, useful buying notes, and a visible enquiry or visit option. Avoid promising live availability unless someone can keep it accurate. If stock changes frequently, say that customers should confirm availability and batch details before travelling. For products where shade or batch consistency matters, explain that customers should check requirements with the dealer and inspect samples where appropriate. Clear limitations prevent misunderstandings better than vague claims. Make the catalogue useful on a phone Organise products by the terms buyers use, not internal shorthand. On each product or category page, include the details you can verify—such as dimensions, finish, material, intended use, brand or model where relevant, and care instructions. Keep units consistent and explain abbreviations. Add a note when information varies by variant or needs confirmation. Use real, representative photos and label them honestly. A screen cannot reliably communicate exact colour, texture or shade, so tell visitors when an image is illustrative and encourage them to check a sample. Compress images so pages remain usable on mobile connections, and make the phone number, location and opening hours easy to find without scrolling through a long description. Turn browsing into a useful visit or enquiry Give visitors a short way to ask about a product: item or category, preferred contact method, and optional context such as quantity or project stage. Do not require sensitive details. Explain whether a request is for a callback, a quote, or confirmation of availability; do not imply that a message reserves stock unless your process actually does that. If customers visit in person, provide a map link and practical directions or parking information only when you can verify them. Choose a build plan you can maintain Before hiring, write a one-page scope: catalogue size, who will add or update products, whether payments are needed, who provides photography and copy, and what should happen when an item is unavailable. Ask each provider to price and demonstrate the same scope, then clarify domain ownership, administrator access, backups, training and ongoing support in writing. A web development firm in India can be assessed with those same questions; compare the answers rather than relying on a broad promise. Before launch, ask someone unfamiliar with the project to find a product category, locate the shop, and send an enquiry on a phone. Fix any step that makes them guess. A small, accurate catalogue that staff can keep current is a better starting point than a large collection of stale listings. Disclosure: I work with MGTech Labs.
