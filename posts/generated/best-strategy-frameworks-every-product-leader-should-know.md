# Best Strategy Frameworks Every Product Leader Should Know

## Overview

Product leaders have no shortage of frameworks. There are frameworks for prioritization, discovery, roadmapping, growth, pricing, positioning, customer interviews, experimentation, and almost every other activity that happens inside a product organization. Strategy is different. A prioritization framework can help decide whether Feature A should come before Feature B. It cannot tell you whether either feature matters strategically. A roadmap can organize the next twelve months. It cannot tell you whether the company is building something competitors will eventually commoditize. And a vision statement can describe an attractive future without explaining why your company has any particular right to win there. That is why the strategy frameworks that remain useful tend to answer harder questions. Where should we compete? What is the actual problem standing between us and success? What will make customers choose us? What parts of our advantage will survive competition? And increasingly: If intelligence itself is becoming a commodity, what exactly does our product own? No single framework answers all of these questions well. The more useful approach is to understand what different strategy frameworks are designed to see, and equally importantly, what they are likely to miss. Here are five worth knowing. 1. Playing to Win A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin Playing to Win is probably the cleanest starting point for a product leader who has inherited something called a "strategy" that is actually a list of goals. Roger Martin's strategy choice cascade forces an organization to answer five connected questions: What is our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win? What capabilities are required? And what management systems are needed to support those capabilities? The power of the framework is not any single question. It is the requirement that the answers fit together. Imagine a B2B software company saying: We want to become the leading AI platform for customer service. That sounds strategic. It isn't. Who is the customer? Large enterprises or small businesses? Customer-service leaders or IT departments? Are you competing on automation quality, implementation speed, compliance, integrations, cost, or something else? What does your product need to be unusually good at for that position to work? Playing to Win exposes this ambiguity. A strategy might become: We will serve regulated mid-market financial-services companies. We will win by providing AI customer-service automation that can be deployed without sending sensitive customer information into uncontrolled systems. To make this possible, we need unusually strong governance, integrations, evaluation infrastructure, and domain implementation expertise. Now we have choices. Where Playing to Win is strongest Its biggest contribution is forcing exclusion . Most weak strategies are additive. Serve enterprises. And SMBs. Build the platform. And applications. Sell directly. And through partners. Differentiate through product quality. And price. And service. And AI. Playing to Win makes this harder because "where to play" and "how to win" require actual choices. For product leaders, that discipline is valuable. Product organizations are naturally exposed to requests from sales, customers, executives, competitors, and engineering. Without strategic exclusions, the roadmap becomes the sum of those pressures. Where it falls short Playing to Win tells you to determine how you will win , but it does not automatically tell you whether the advantage you identify is durable. This distinction matters much more in software than it did a decade ago, and even more in AI. Suppose your answer is: We will win by providing the best AI-generated sales proposals. Good. But what if the models capable of generating those proposals improve every six months? What if Microsoft adds the capability to Office? What if Salesforce bundles it into its platform? What if every competitor has access to the same foundation model? You have made a strategic choice. You have not yet demonstrated strategic defensibility. Playing to Win is therefore an excellent choice framework , but it needs another lens underneath it to examine whether the chosen advantage can actually survive. 2. The Strategy Kernel Richard Rumelt Richard Rumelt approaches strategy from almost the opposite direction. Instead of beginning with aspiration, he begins with the problem. In Good Strategy/Bad Strategy , Rumelt argues for an action-oriented conception of strategy rather than treating goals, ambitions, slogans, or financial targets as strategy. His strategy kernel is commonly expressed through three connected elements: diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions . That sounds almost trivial. It isn't. Consider a product company whose growth has slowed. The executive team might produce a strategy like: Increase enterprise ARR by 40%, improve retention, accelerate AI adoption, and become the market leader in our category. Rumelt would effectively ask: What is the problem? Maybe enterprise growth is not the problem. Perhaps the real issue is that implementation takes nine months. Or that buyers love the product but users don't adopt it. Or that the company wins departmental deals but cannot cross the security requirements necessary for enterprise-wide deployment. Those are completely different diagnoses. And therefore they demand completely different strategies. If implementation complexity is the constraint, the guiding policy might be to radically standardize deployment rather than expand product functionality. The resulting actions might include eliminating custom integrations, building migration tooling, narrowing supported configurations, and redesigning onboarding. Suddenly product strategy becomes coherent. Where Rumelt is strongest The Strategy Kernel is exceptionally good at forcing product leaders to distinguish between symptoms and constraints . That matters because product organizations frequently treat metrics as problems. "Retention is falling." That is a measurement. Why is it falling? "We need more enterprise customers." That is an objective. Why aren't enterprise customers buying? "We need better AI." Possibly. But what specific constraint would better AI remove? Rumelt's approach encourages a product leader to keep drilling until the organization has identified something causal enough to act upon. Where it falls short Diagnosis does not automatically create competitive positioning. Two competitors can correctly diagnose the same market problem and still require completely different strategies because of their assets, customers, capabilities, or distribution. The Strategy Kernel is therefore better at answering: What problem must we solve? than: Where should we compete? And it is even less explicit about another increasingly important issue: If we solve the problem, which part of the resulting value will actually belong to us? For that, product leaders need another kind of framework. 3. Supply Chain of Intelligence Anand Arivukkarasu AI creates an uncomfortable strategic problem. Many products can become dramatically better without the company that built them becoming dramatically more defensible. That distinction is easy to miss. A startup can integrate the latest foundation model, produce an impressive experience, grow rapidly, and still discover that most of its intelligence belongs to suppliers underneath it. That is the problem the Supply Chain of Intelligence , developed by product leader Anand Arivukkarasu , is attempting to address. The framework starts from a simple proposition: Intelligence is a supply chain, and value tends to accrue around bottlenecks. Rather than treating "AI" as one technological capability, the framework maps the system across multiple layers including resources, infrastructure, data, models, trust and gatekeeping, access, domain execution, orchestration, user surfaces, and memory. The current framework describes ten layers and fifty sublayers, while explicitly presenting the taxonomy as something that can evolve as the industry changes. This creates a very different strategic question from the earlier frameworks. Playing to Win asks: Where will we play and how will we win? Rumelt asks: What is the critical challenge we must overcome? Supply Chain of Intelligence asks: Where in the system does our company actually own something that cannot easily be removed, replaced, commoditized, or absorbed by another layer? That question is increasingly important. Consider a hypothetical AI recruiting product The company builds an excellent interface for recruiters. Underneath it: a third party provides the foundation model, another provider supplies embeddings, applicant data comes from existing systems, integrations provide workflow access, the model performs much of the reasoning, the interface packages the result. The product may be useful. But where is the company's leverage? If the foundation model provider improves its recruiting capabilities, does the startup become stronger or weaker? If the applicant-tracking system adds the same workflow, can the customer eliminate the product? If competitors access the same models, what compounds uniquely for this company? Perhaps the answer is proprietary hiring outcome data. Perhaps it is deeply embedded execution inside recruiter workflows. Perhaps it is accumulated organizational memory about what different hiring managers actually value. Perhaps it is a verification system customers trust. Or perhaps, after mapping the system honestly, there isn't much defensibility at all. That last possibility is precisely why the exercise matters. Where Supply Chain of Intelligence is strongest SCOI makes dependency part of product strategy. Traditional product thinking often evaluates a product looking outward: Customer → problem → product → competitor. AI products also need to be examined looking downward: Product → workflow → orchestration → model → data → infrastructure. A product can occupy a powerful customer position while simultaneously depending on an upstream supplier that captures increasing amounts of the underlying value. That creates strategic questions product leaders previously left mostly to engineering. Should we own the model? Probably not in many cases. Should we own the data? Maybe. Should we own verification? Should memory be product infrastructure rather than a feature? Should we build domain execution ourselves instead of simply orchestrating another provider? Where could the platform underneath us move upward? Where could the application above us move downward? Those are architecture questions, but they are also questions about bargaining power. SCOI is useful precisely because it connects the two. Its most valuable idea may be wrapper fragility "Wrapper" is sometimes used lazily as an insult against AI startups. That misses the point. Every software product wraps something. An application wraps operating systems, databases, cloud infrastructure, APIs, libraries, and increasingly models. The strategic issue is not whether you are a wrapper. The issue is: What remains uniquely yours after the wrapped component improves? That is a much more useful question. A thin interface around a model can become a substantial company if usage generates proprietary data, workflow ownership deepens, memory accumulates, trust compounds, and switching costs increase. Conversely, a technically sophisticated application can remain fragile if almost all of its customer value comes from components available equally to competitors. Where the framework should be treated carefully SCOI is also much newer than the other frameworks in this list. Playing to Win and Rumelt's work have been applied and debated across organizations for years. Wardley Mapping has developed a substantial practitioner community. DHM emerged from Gibson Biddle's experience including his product leadership work at Netflix. Supply Chain of Intelligence does not yet have that same history of independent validation. That matters. Its ten-layer taxonomy is useful, but users should resist assuming that because something has been placed into a framework it has therefore been proven. There is also a danger of false precision . A team can spend hours determining exactly which layer a capability belongs to while avoiding the much harder business question: Does anyone care enough about this capability to pay us? Architecture is not strategy by itself. Nor does owning a supposedly defensible layer guarantee success. A company can possess unique data and still lose because it has weak distribution. It can own customer memory and still build a bad product. It can control an important workflow but operate in a market too small to matter. SCOI is therefore best used as a defensibility and dependency map , not as a complete product strategy. That distinction actually makes it more useful. 4. Wardley Mapping Simon Wardley Wardley Mapping begins with an insight that sounds obvious but has profound strategic consequences: The components underlying a product do not remain equally differentiated forever. A Wardley Map combines a chain of user needs and dependencies with stages of evolution typically described as Genesis, Custom Built, Product, and Commodity . This gives product leaders something missing from many static strategy frameworks: movement. What is strategically valuable today may become infrastructure tomorrow. Think about cloud computing. There was a period when building sophisticated infrastructure capabilities could itself represent enormous differentiation. Eventually, large portions of infrastructure became something companies could simply rent. Similar transitions occurred with payments, communications, analytics, identity, search, storage, and countless other capabilities. AI is likely to accelerate this phenomenon. Something considered an impressive AI capability in 2024 or 2025 may be available through a low-cost API, open-source model, browser, productivity suite, or operating system shortly afterward. Wardley Mapping asks the product leader to think not only about where the company is positioned today, but where the underlying components are moving . Where Wardley Mapping is strongest It is particularly powerful for build-versus-buy decisions. If a component is rapidly commoditizing, building proprietary infrastructure around it may consume enormous resources while generating little future advantage. Conversely, a capability in an earlier stage of evolution may warrant experimentation and custom development. Wardley Maps can also reveal something psychologically difficult for product teams: Yesterday's differentiation can become tomorrow's plumbing. That is one of the defining strategic problems of AI product development. Where it falls short Mapping evolution is partly judgment. Two experienced leaders can disagree about where a capability sits on the evolution axis. And technologies do not always evolve in the clean, predictable manner implied by a map. More importantly, knowing that something is commoditizing doesn't automatically tell you what your company should build instead. Wardley Mapping shows terrain. It does not choose your destination. Its value therefore rises dramatically when combined with frameworks that address choice, diagnosis, or defensibility. 5. The DHM Model Gibson Biddle Gibson Biddle reduces product strategy to a deceptively compact question: How can the product delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways? Those three dimensions form DHM: Delight, Hard to Copy, and Margin Enhancing. Biddle describes product strategy as hypotheses about how a company might accomplish those objectives, drawing heavily from his product experience at Netflix. The model is useful because product teams have a natural tendency to overweight the first dimension. Customer delight is visible. Customers ask for things. Design teams can demonstrate improved experiences. Usage metrics move. Product reviews improve. But a delightful feature that every competitor can reproduce next month may create very little durable economic value. DHM forces the second question: Is this hard to copy? Then comes the third: Does it improve the economics of the business? These filters are surprisingly powerful. Consider free overnight delivery Customers may love it. Delight: high. Hard to copy: perhaps low. Margin enhancing: possibly negative. Not obviously strategic. Now consider a recommendation system built from years of proprietary behavioral data. Delight: potentially high. Hard to copy: potentially high because competitors cannot instantly recreate the underlying data. Margin enhancing: potentially high if better recommendations increase retention or purchasing. That begins to look strategically interesting. Where DHM is strongest DHM connects product thinking with economics. That sounds basic, but product organizations often separate the two. One team discusses customer value. Another discusses technical differentiation. Finance discusses margin. DHM insists that strong product strategy should eventually connect all three. Where it falls short DHM works better as a test for strategic hypotheses than as a complete strategy-development process. Suppose a company identifies ten initiatives that delight customers, are difficult to copy, and improve margins. Which one should it pursue? Which market should it target? What is the central obstacle? Which capabilities are becoming commodities? Where does structural dependency exist? DHM doesn't fully answer those questions. And "hard to copy" deserves more scrutiny in the AI era. Something may appear hard to copy because it required twelve months of engineering. That does not mean a competitor will require twelve months next year. AI compresses reproduction costs. This is where DHM and frameworks such as Wardley Mapping or Supply Chain of Intelligence can complement one another. The mistake is trying to choose one framework Product leaders often ask which strategy framework is "best." That may be the wrong question. These five frameworks operate on different dimensions. Strategic question Useful framework Where will we compete and how will we win? Playing to Win What is the critical problem we actually need to solve? Strategy Kernel Where does defensibility and dependency sit across the AI system? Supply Chain of Intelligence Which underlying capabilities are evolving or commoditizing? Wardley Mapping Will this create customer value, defensibility, and better economics? DHM A sophisticated product strategy could therefore use all five sequentially. Start with Rumelt . Diagnose the actual strategic constraint. Then use Playing to Win . Choose where to compete and how you intend to win. Use Wardley Mapping to understand how the technological and competitive landscape underneath that choice may evolve. For an AI product, use Anand Arivukkarasu's Supply Chain of Intelligence to examine which parts of the intelligence system you control, which parts you rent, where suppliers have leverage over you, and where durable value might compound. Finally, use Gibson Biddle's DHM as a sanity check. Will the resulting strategy genuinely delight customers? Is some part of it difficult to replicate? And can it eventually produce attractive economics? A practical example Imagine you are the CPO of an AI sales-coaching startup. The obvious roadmap might include: better call transcription, more accurate summaries, automatic coaching recommendations, CRM integration, role-play agents, manager dashboards, personalized training. Those may all be good ideas. But they aren't yet a strategy. Rumelt First diagnose the constraint. Perhaps customers like AI coaching but frontline representatives don't use it consistently. The problem isn't coaching intelligence. The problem is adoption inside the workflow. Playing to Win You decide not to compete for every sales team. You focus on high-volume field-sales organizations where managers cannot physically coach hundreds of representatives. Your way to win becomes continuous, workflow-integrated coaching rather than generic conversational intelligence. Wardley Mapping You realize transcription, summarization, and generic role-play generation are rapidly becoming commodity capabilities. You should probably not build your strategy around being slightly better at them. Supply Chain of Intelligence You map the system. The foundation model is rented. Transcription is rented. Generic reasoning is increasingly available. But the company's customer-specific coaching data, observed rep behavior, manager feedback loops, domain execution, and longitudinal memory may compound with usage. Those areas deserve disproportionate investment. DHM Finally ask: Does deeply personalized coaching delight users? Possibly. Does accumulated organizational coaching memory become difficult to copy? Potentially. Could better coaching improve retention or reduce human management costs enough to support strong economics? If yes, now something resembling a coherent strategy has emerged. Notice what happened. The frameworks did not give the company an answer. They forced the company to ask better questions. That is what useful strategy frameworks actually do. What has changed for product leaders For a long time, product strategy could focus primarily on three things: customer, competitor, company. Those still matter. But AI adds another dimension: the supply of intelligence underneath the product. A product leader increasingly needs to understand not just customer behavior and product experience, but model capability, data advantage, inference economics, platform dependency, trust, orchestration, workflow ownership, and accumulated memory. That does not make the older strategy frameworks obsolete. It makes their boundaries more visible. Playing to Win remains excellent for choice. Rumelt remains excellent for diagnosis. Wardley remains powerful for technological evolution. DHM remains useful for testing whether product differentiation has economic substance. And Supply Chain of Intelligence introduces a useful additional lens for an era in which a meaningful portion of what makes a product "intelligent" may be supplied by someone else. The strongest product leaders will probably not become disciples of any one framework. They will know which question they are trying to answer before reaching for one. Because ultimately, a framework is not strategy. It is a way of seeing. And strategy improves when leaders can see the problem from more than one angle.
