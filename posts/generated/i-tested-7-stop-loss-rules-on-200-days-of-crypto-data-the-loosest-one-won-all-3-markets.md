# I tested 7 stop-loss rules on 200 days of crypto data. The loosest one won all 3 markets.

## Overview

Every trading tutorial gives you the same advice: always use a stop-loss, and keep it tight. So I did the boring thing and measured it. Seven stop-loss rules, six months of real Binance candles, three pairs, one entry signal, and a fill model that checks stops against 5-minute highs and lows instead of the 15-minute close. The result was the opposite of the advice: the wider I made the stop, the more money the bot made (or, more accurately, the less it lost). The tightest stop produced the most trades and the worst returns. And out of 21 rule/pair combinations, zero beat simply buying and holding. This is the writeup, the code, and what I actually changed in my live-config Freqtrade bot afterwards. The setup Data: BTC/USDT , ETH/USDT , SOL/USDT on Binance, 15-minute candles, 2026-03-09 → 2026-09-25 (200 days, 19,256 bars per pair). I also pulled the 5-minute candles for the same window (57,770 bars per pair) so I could resolve whether a stop was hit inside a 15-minute candle rather than assuming the close is the truth. Entry signal: one line, no indicator soup. Long when RSI(14) < 30 on the 15-minute close. One position at a time, no pyramiding, no shorting, no leverage. Exit: the only thing that changed between runs. All seven rules share a hard 24-hour maximum hold (96 × 15m bars), so "do nothing until you get stopped out" can't silently become the winning strategy. Fees: 0.1% per side, subtracted from every single trade. That's 0.2% round trip, and with 200+ trades per rule it matters a lot more than the stop placement does. I ran the whole thing with pandas offline — no exchange calls, no per-tick data, reproducible in about a minute. The advice is exactly backwards for a mean-reversion entry. 2. The tightest stop traded the most and earned the least. TP 1% / SL 0.5% fired 235, 306 and 304 times. Every extra trade paid 0.2% in fees, and the 0.5% stop converted normal noise into realised losses. On ETH it took 306 trades to lose 24% — a very expensive way to be right 41% of the time. 3. The ATR stop — the "smart, volatility-aware" choice — was the worst on BTC (−38.83%) with a 24.8% win rate. A 2 × ATR14 stop on 15-minute BTC candles sits around 1–1.5%, which is functionally a tight stop. Naming a stop "adaptive" doesn't make it wide. 4. Nothing beat buy-and-hold. Not one of the 21 runs. The three positive runs all happened on SOL, where buy-and-hold returned +43.44% — so the bot that "won" gave up 35 points of upside and took on 100 trades of execution risk to do it. Why this happens The stop-loss is not an edge. It cannot be. Your entry signal determines which trades you take; the stop only decides how a losing trade is distributed over time . A tight stop makes the equity curve smooth, the win rate look plausible, and the drawdown small in the backtest — while guaranteeing that anything that goes against you even slightly is locked in as a loss. The real problem here is upstream: RSI < 30 on trending crypto is not a mean-reversion signal, it's an early-exit signal. In a market that goes up 25–43% over the window, buying every oversold dip and bailing out at the first 2% wiggle is a machine for paying fees. Adding a tighter stop to a signal with no edge is optimisation of a negative. I proved that twice in the same 200 days: the exit rule that "won" still lost to not trading at all. What I changed in the bot Concretely, in config.json for the Freqtrade bot: "stoploss": -0.10 — the default is 10% for a reason. If your signal needs a 0.5% stop to survive, the signal is the problem, not the stop. "trailing_stop": true with "trailing_stop_positive": 0.02 and "trailing_stop_positive_offset": 0.03 . The stop only tightens after the trade is 3% in profit. This is what actually beat the static tight stop on SOL (−9.63% vs −34.98% for TP 3% / SL 1.5%). "use_exit_signal": true and never let a stop be the primary exit. In this dataset, exit_reason == roi trades were the profitable ones; the stop_loss exits were where the money went. Fees in the backtest config. Freqtrade has a "fee" field in the backtest config — set it to 0.001 and leave it. A rule that only wins with fees at zero is a rule that loses on the exchange. And the meta-lesson, which cost me the least time and saved the most: measure the exit rule against buy-and-hold, not against the other exit rules. Seven rules can fight each other forever while all of them are losing to a token you never sold.
