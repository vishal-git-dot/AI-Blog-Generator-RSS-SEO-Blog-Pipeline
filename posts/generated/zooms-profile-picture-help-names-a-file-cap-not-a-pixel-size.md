# Zoom's Profile Picture Help Names a File Cap, Not a Pixel Size

## Overview

Zoom's help article for the web-portal profile picture, loaded on 27 November 2026, tells you the file types and a size cap. It does not tell you how many pixels to export. The page is titled Managing your profile picture on the Zoom web portal . Its schema.org dateModified is 2026-10-30 10:53:57. The only requirement I can quote is this: "Supported file types include JPG/JPEG, GIF, or PNG files smaller than 2MB." The add and change steps repeat that sentence, and they mention an optional crop area. They do not mention a width, a minimum edge, a recommended square, or a circular display. There is a second official number, and it does not agree. Release notes for Web , fetched the same day, have a schema.org dateModified of 2026-11-23 11:55:32. Under the heading "October 19, 2026," a general feature titled "Profile picture size limit increase" says users can upload profile photos "up to 10MB instead of the previous 2MB limit." That entry even links back to the help article. The help article, whose dateModified is eleven days later, still prints "smaller than 2MB." I am not going to pick a winner. I pointed the product link at ProfessionalHeadshot, an AI professional headshot generator, and the short page it keeps on zoom profile picture size . That generator typically returns forty to a hundred professional headshots from a small set of uploaded photos, usually in under an hour. I did not run it on the fixtures below. The landing page does recommend a square of at least 400×400 pixels, and 800×800 or higher for sharper display, and it describes the on-screen photo as a circle. Those are the product page's words, fetched today. They are not official Zoom specifications, and I did not measure live Zoom CSS. The measurement I can actually run is local: export a square, apply an inscribed circular mask, and see how much of the square is lost, which features get clipped, and whether common export sizes still read after that crop. Nothing here was uploaded to Zoom, and nothing was sent through the product. A local inscribed circle, not a client claim The geometry does not depend on Zoom. For a W×W square, an inscribed circle of diameter W keeps π/4 of the area, about 78.54 percent, and discards the four corners. The largest axis-aligned square that still fits inside that circle has side W/√2, about 0.7071 W, and occupies half of the original square. Content inside that inner square is fully visible. Content in the four corner wedges is gone. The four side lobes between the inner square and the circle stay in the picture, but they sit close to the edge. I built two 2048-pixel fixtures in Pillow 13.0.0. Neither fixture is a camera portrait of anyone. The first is a geometric badge: concentric rings at 0.50, 1/√2, 0.85, and 0.95 of the radius, mid-side ticks just inside the circle, a stroke around that inner safe square, and purple chevrons in the four corners. The second is a face-like stand-in made of ovals: a wide shoulder mass, a darker hair mass, a head oval slightly above mid-frame, and two small ellipses plus a short bar as features. I stored a label map for head, hair, shoulders, and features so clip fractions come from known pixels, not from a later guess. The circular mask used for counts is a hard threshold on distance from the pixel center: a pixel is inside if that distance is at most W/2. The figures use a slightly antialiased mask so the edge is not jaggy, but the numbers below always use the hard mask. Every resize uses LANCZOS, passed explicitly. Pillow's Image.resize documentation, fetched today, defaults to BICUBIC when the filter is omitted, and I did not want that default silently mixed in. How much of the square actually goes away At 2048×2048, the hard mask kept 3,294,288 of 4,194,304 pixels, or 78.5419 percent. That is 0.002 percentage points above π/4 (78.5398 percent). The 900,016 pixels outside the circle are 21.46 percent of the square. At the other export sizes I used, the measured fraction stayed between 78.53 and 78.69 percent; the coarsest grid, 128 pixels, was the farthest from the ideal, as you would expect. On the badge, the corner chevrons were a complete loss: all 10,862 of those pixels sat outside the circle. The mid-side ticks were a complete keep: all 2,624 pixels stayed in. Every ring, including the 0.95 ring, was fully inside at this resolution. The safe-square stroke was almost a complete keep, and the four pixels that leaked are the interesting part. The corners of that inner square lie on the circle. A stroke drawn on the square therefore puts a few pixels just outside the hard mask. Of 46,144 stroke pixels, four were clipped, a fraction of 0.0087 percent. If you treat the inner square as a "safe zone," keep the important content slightly inside it, not on the line. Figure 1. A wide source loses the sides before the circle does any work If the source is not already square, a center crop to 1:1 happens first. I placed the same 2048-pixel stand-in onto a wider canvas, then took the centered square, then applied the circle. A 16:9 canvas at height 2048 is 3641×2048 after int(round(2048 * 16 / 9)) . The side strips removed by the square crop are 1,593 pixels of width, or 1,593/3,641 of the canvas, 43.75 percent. A 4:3 canvas at the same height is 2731×2048; the square crop discards 683/2,731 of it, 25.01 percent. Those fractions are a little above the exact 7/16 and 1/4 because the rounded widths are 3641 and 2731, not 3640.889 and 2730.667. After that square crop, the remaining frame was the same centered stand-in I started with, so the circle's effect did not change: head and hair still unclipped, shoulders still 30.38 percent gone. The wide-source loss is in the side strips, not in an extra bite from the circle. If you start from a 16:9 still and let a center square crop do the work, you have already thrown away more than two fifths of the file before the circular mask is applied. That is a reason to square the frame yourself, with the subject where you want it, rather than hoping a later crop will guess. Common export sizes, and the official file cap I exported both fixtures at 128, 256, 400, 800, 1024, 1080, and 2048 pixels. Each size was written as a PNG, and also as a JPEG at qualities 85 and 70 with 4:2:0 chroma (Pillow subsampling=2 ). The 400 and 800 sizes are in the list because the product page names them, not because Zoom published them. 128 and 256 are undersized on purpose. 1024, 1080, and 2048 are larger squares people actually export. Every file I wrote sat comfortably under both official caps, under either a 10^6 or a 2^20 reading of "MB." The largest was the 2048-pixel badge JPEG at quality 85: 257,730 bytes, about 13 percent of 2,000,000 and about 12 percent of 2 MiB. The smallest was the 128-pixel stand-in JPEG at quality 70: 2,119 bytes. On these drawings, the 2MB figure printed on the help page was not the binding constraint. I did not encode a camera photograph, and I am not claiming a noisy 2048-pixel PNG from a real camera would land in the same place. PNG byte size also did not grow monotonically with the edge length. The 2048-pixel badge PNG, which is the original drawing with large flat fills, weighed 51,411 bytes. After a LANCZOS resize, the 800-pixel PNG occupied 167,802 bytes, and the 1080-pixel PNG occupied 230,373 bytes. Downsampling introduced more unique edge colors than the source had, and those mid-size files compressed worse. I would not use PNG length as a proxy for "how much image is there." What survives when the circle is later shown small I still do not have an official display diameter, so I will not pretend 96 or 400 pixels is "the Zoom tile." I did run a local what-if: take each export, resize it with LANCZOS to 96, 160, 240, or 400 pixels, and measure the peak-to-peak luminance in a thin band around the 0.95-radius ring, against a native export at that same display size. That metric is coarse. A soft ring can still have a wide min-to-max if some dark and light pixels remain, so I am not treating the ratio as a quality score. The useful reading is narrower. At a 400-pixel display, the 128-pixel export, which has to be upsampled, kept a 0.95-ring contrast of 200 against a native 400-pixel contrast of 242, a ratio of 0.826. Exports of 400 and 2048 both measured 242. The 800-pixel export measured 241. Once the export was at least the display size, this particular ring was essentially intact. The 128-pixel file was the one that had to be enlarged, and it was the one that lagged. The visual comparison is clearer than the contrast ratio.
