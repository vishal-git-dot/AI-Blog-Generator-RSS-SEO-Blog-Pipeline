# Building a Global eSIM Platform: Why I Chose This Stack Over Traditional SIM Cards

## Overview

The Problem: Why Physical SIM Cards Are Broken I've traveled to 30+ countries, and every single time, the same ritual: land at the airport, find a SIM card kiosk, show my passport, wait 20 minutes for activation, and pray the data actually works. It's 2026, and we're still doing this. Physical SIM cards are a relic of 1991 — a piece of plastic with a chip that locks you into one carrier, one country, one plan. If you're a digital nomad hopping between Thailand, Vietnam, and Indonesia, you're buying three different SIMs. If you're a business traveler doing London → Dubai → Singapore, you're either paying $10/day roaming fees or juggling SIM cards like a street magician. The numbers are staggering: the global travel SIM market is worth over $10 billion, and most of it runs on physical cards distributed through airport kiosks and convenience stores. The friction is real — and it's entirely unnecessary. So I decided to build iWanteSIM , a global eSIM platform that lets travelers buy data plans instantly, activate them in under 60 seconds, and stay connected across 200+ countries — without ever touching a physical SIM card. Here's the full story of the tech stack, the architecture decisions, and the carrier integration nightmares I didn't see coming. The Core Architecture: What Powers a Global eSIM Platform When I started architecting this, I knew the stack had to handle three things really well: Real-time eSIM provisioning — generating and delivering eSIM profiles to users' devices within seconds of purchase Multi-carrier orchestration — talking to dozens of mobile network operators (MNOs) across 200+ countries, each with their own API quirks Global coverage routing — intelligently selecting the best carrier profile based on the user's destination, device compatibility, and real-time network availability Here's the stack I landed on after months of prototyping: Backend: Node.js + PostgreSQL + Redis I chose Node.js for the API layer because eSIM provisioning is inherently I/O-bound. You're making API calls to carrier partners, waiting for SM-DP+ (Subscription Manager Data Preparation) servers to generate profiles, and handling webhooks for activation status. Node's event loop is perfect for this — non-blocking I/O means one server can handle thousands of concurrent provisioning requests without breaking a sweat. PostgreSQL handles the relational data: user accounts, orders, eSIM profiles (ICCID, activation codes, QR payloads), carrier contracts, and pricing tables. The schema is normalized but denormalized where it matters — the esim_profiles table is the hot path, queried on every purchase and activation. Redis is the secret sauce. eSIM profile generation takes 2-8 seconds depending on the carrier, so I pre-generate and cache profiles in Redis. When a user buys a plan for Thailand, we pop a pre-provisioned profile from the queue, assign it to their order, and deliver the QR code instantly. No waiting. The cache also holds rate-limit counters for carrier APIs (some MNOs cap you at 100 requests/minute), session tokens, and real-time activation status. Frontend: Next.js + Tailwind The customer-facing side is Next.js with server-side rendering for SEO (critical for an e-commerce platform). The checkout flow is a single-page experience: select country → pick plan → pay → get QR code. Tailwind keeps the UI clean and responsive — most users are on mobile, often on spotty airport Wi-Fi, so the bundle has to be lean. The QR code rendering uses qrcode (82M monthly npm downloads) with error correction level H (30% recovery) — because if a traveler's screen is cracked or dim, that QR still needs to scan. The Hard Part: Carrier Integrations This is where things got real. Integrating with mobile carriers is nothing like integrating with Stripe or Twilio. The telecom industry runs on protocols designed in the 1990s — GSMA specifications like SGP.22 (consumer eSIM) and SGP.32 (IoT eSIM) are dense, 300-page documents written by committee. I evaluated three approaches: Option 1: Direct MNO Integration Sign individual contracts with every carrier in every country. This gives you the best margins but requires legal teams, local entities, and months of negotiation per carrier. For 200+ countries, this is a multi-year, multi-million-dollar undertaking. Hard pass for a startup. Option 2: eSIM Aggregators (Gigs, 1Global, Celitech) These companies act as middlemen — they've already done the carrier deals and expose a unified API. You call one endpoint to provision an eSIM for any supported country, and they handle the carrier-specific complexity behind the scenes. Gigs — Great API design, developer-first documentation, supports smartphones and wearables. Their API lets you create subscriptions, manage plans, and handle porting. Pricing is transparent but margins are thinner since they take a cut. 1Global — Massive coverage (190+ countries, 600+ networks), runs their own core network infrastructure. They're more enterprise-focused with IoT/M2M support and compliance features (MiFID recording for financial services). Celitech — Built specifically for travel providers (OTAs, airlines, hotels). SOC 2 certified, US-hosted, and designed for embedding eSIM sales into existing booking flows. Their API is literally "one line of code" for basic integration. Option 3: Hybrid — What I Actually Built I went with a hybrid model. For the MVP, I integrated with two eSIM aggregators as primary providers, giving me instant coverage across 200+ countries without signing a single carrier contract. The aggregator API abstracts away the GSMA protocol complexity — I don't need to understand SM-DP+ server addressing or profile type coding. But here's the key architectural decision: I built an abstraction layer between my platform and the aggregators. Every carrier partner (aggregator or direct MNO) implements a common interface: interface ESimProvider { getCoverage (): Promise < Country [] > ; getPlans ( country : string ): Promise < Plan [] > ; provisionProfile ( plan : Plan , user : User ): Promise < EsimProfile > ; getActivationStatus ( iccid : string ): Promise < ActivationStatus > ; getUsage ( iccid : string ): Promise < DataUsage > ; } This means I can swap aggregators, add direct carrier relationships, or A/B test providers without touching the rest of the platform. Today I'm using aggregators for speed; tomorrow I can negotiate direct MNO deals for better margins and plug them in through the same interface. The lesson: never couple your business logic to a third-party API . Telecom APIs change, carriers get acquired, aggregators raise prices. The abstraction layer is cheap to build upfront and priceless when you need to pivot. Real-Time Provisioning: The 60-Second Challenge My goal was simple: from "I want an eSIM" to "I'm connected" in under 60 seconds. Here's what actually happens behind the scenes when a user buys a plan: Order received (0-100ms) — API validates the order, checks inventory, reserves a pre-cached eSIM profile from Redis Payment processed (100-500ms) — Stripe handles the payment; webhook confirms success Profile provisioning (500ms-8s) — API calls the aggregator to activate the eSIM profile for the user's device. This is the bottleneck — some carriers are fast (2s), others take 8s because their SM-DP+ servers are, frankly, ancient QR code delivery (immediate) — User receives a QR code (LPA-compliant) via email and on-screen. Scanning it triggers the eSIM download on their device Activation confirmation (5-30s after scan) — Webhook from the carrier confirms the profile is active. User is online. The pre-caching strategy in Redis was the breakthrough. Without it, every purchase would trigger a synchronous provisioning call that takes 2-8 seconds — unacceptable for a checkout flow. With pre-caching, the user waits only for payment processing, and provisioning happens asynchronously. By the time they finish reading the "How to install your eSIM" instructions, the profile is ready. API Design Decisions I'd Make Again 1. Idempotency Keys on Every Mutation When you're dealing with carrier APIs that sometimes timeout without confirming whether a profile was created, idempotency is non-negotiable. Every order creation, profile provisioning, and activation request carries an idempotency key. If a request fails and retries, the carrier recognizes the key and returns the original result instead of creating a duplicate profile (and charging me twice). 2. Webhook-First Architecture Carrier activation status doesn't change on a predictable schedule. Polling every 30 seconds wastes API quota and adds latency. Instead, the platform exposes webhook endpoints that carriers call when a profile activates, deactivates, or hits a data threshold. The internal event bus (Redis pub/sub) fans out these events to update user dashboards, send email notifications, and trigger usage alerts. 3. Graceful Degradation If a carrier's API is down (it happens more often than you'd think), the platform doesn't fail. It falls back to a secondary provider for that country, or queues the order for retry with exponential backoff. Users see "Your eSIM is being prepared — we'll notify you when it's ready" instead of an error page. This alone reduced support tickets by 40%. What I Got Wrong (And Fixed) No building-in-public story is complete without the failures: Mistake #1: Underestimating carrier API inconsistency. I assumed RESTful APIs with consistent JSON responses. Reality: one carrier returns activation status as "ACTIVE" , another returns 1 , a third returns {"state": "activated", "timestamp": ...} . I now run every carrier response through a normalization layer that maps all variants to a canonical format. This is tedious but essential. Mistake #2: Not planning for eSIM profile exhaustion. Each eSIM profile (ICCID) is a finite resource. Aggregators allocate a pool of profiles per partner, and when you run out, provisioning fails silently. I learned this the hard way during a traffic spike from a travel blog feature — 200 orders failed because the profile pool was empty. Now there's a monitoring dashboard with alerts at 20% and 50% pool depletion, plus automatic top-up requests to the aggregator. Mistake #3: QR code rendering on low-end devices. The first version used a canvas-based QR renderer that broke on older Android phones common in Southeast Asian markets. Switched to server-side QR generation with SVG fallback. Works everywhere now. Why This Stack Over Traditional SIM Cards? The traditional SIM card supply chain looks like this: manufacturer → distributor → retailer → customer. Each step adds cost, delay, and plastic waste. An eSIM platform collapses that into: API → device. No manufacturing, no shipping, no inventory, no landfill. For the business, the unit economics are transformative. A physical SIM costs $0.50-2.00 to manufacture, package, and distribute. An eSIM profile costs fractions of a cent to generate. The margin difference alone makes the platform viable at scale. For users, the experience is night and day. Instead of hunting for a SIM card shop in a foreign airport at 2 AM, they buy a plan on their phone before takeoff and activate it the moment they land. What's Next The platform is live and processing orders across 200+ countries, but there's a long roadmap ahead: Direct carrier partnerships — Moving from 100% aggregator-dependent to a mix of direct MNO relationships for better margins on high-volume routes (Europe, Southeast Asia, North America) IoT/M2M support — eSIM isn't just for phones. Connected cars, smart meters, and asset trackers all need cellular connectivity, and the SGP.32 standard makes IoT eSIM provisioning viable Usage-based pricing — Most eSIM plans today are fixed-duration (7 days, 30 days). Pay-as-you-go data, like AWS for connectivity, is the endgame Open-source the abstraction layer — The provider interface I built could help other developers integrate with eSIM aggregators without reinventing the wheel The Bottom Line Building an eSIM platform is 20% standard web development and 80% telecom industry wrangling. The tech stack (Node.js, PostgreSQL, Redis, Next.js) is straightforward — any competent full-stack developer could build the scaffolding in a week. The hard part is navigating carrier relationships, understanding GSMA specifications, and building resilience against API failures that are outside your control. But the opportunity is massive.
