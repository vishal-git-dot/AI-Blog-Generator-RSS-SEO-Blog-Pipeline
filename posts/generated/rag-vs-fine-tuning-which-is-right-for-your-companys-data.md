# RAG vs Fine-Tuning: Which Is Right for Your Company's Data?

## Overview

In short: For most companies, RAG is the right place to start. With RAG, the model looks up your documents at the moment it answers, cites the source, and stays current without retraining. Fine-tuning is for changing how the model behaves (a fixed format, a brand voice, repetitive high-volume tasks), not for memorizing data that changes. If you do need it, consider an open-weight model deployed in your own cloud. RAG and fine-tuning in one sentence: what does each do with your data? RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): before answering, the system searches your documents for the relevant passages and hands them to the model along with the question. The model itself doesn't change. What changes is what it reads on each query. Fine-tuning: you retrain a model on your own examples to adjust its internal parameters. The change is permanent: the model learns a format, a tone or a specific task. Think of it this way. RAG is giving an employee access to the company files and asking them to say which document each answer comes from. Fine-tuning is training that employee until a certain job becomes second nature. The first is about knowing . The second is about doing things a certain way . Many bad decisions start by confusing these two needs. What's the difference between RAG and fine-tuning? In a study presented at EMNLP 2024, RAG consistently outperformed unsupervised fine-tuning , both for knowledge the model had already seen and for entirely new knowledge. The same work shows that models struggle to learn new facts through unsupervised fine-tuning unless they're exposed to many variations of the same fact. It also warns that training can cause the model to lose some of the knowledge it had before . Before training: are better prompts and examples enough? Often, yes. RAG is the natural choice when any of these apply: The information changes. Prices, catalogs, internal policies, inventory or active contracts. Retraining a model every time a rate changes makes no sense. You need to know where the answer came from. In contractual, regulatory or quality matters, an answer without a source is useless. With RAG, the user opens the cited document and checks. Not everyone can see everything. A sales rep shouldn't get answers based on payroll, and one client shouldn't see another client's documents. RAG filters which documents are searched based on the user. Your documents are heterogeneous. Manuals, PDFs, spreadsheets and databases can be integrated without turning them into training datasets. You want to validate fast. A pilot on a limited set of documents can be built and measured in weeks. Typical use cases: an internal assistant that answers questions about operating procedures, a support agent that looks up resolved tickets, or a WhatsApp AI agent that handles sales with an up-to-date catalog . Why we talk about hybrid RAG Semantic search with embeddings finds passages by meaning. For example, “how do I return a product?” finds the returns policy even if it doesn't use those words. But it struggles with exact data such as SKU codes, contract numbers or proper names. Keyword search is good at exactly that. Hybrid RAG combines both, so it can handle natural-language questions as well as queries with precise identifiers. When should you fine-tune a model? Fine-tuning makes sense when the problem is about behavior , not knowledge: A fixed, strict output format. For example, always extracting the same fields from invoices, classifying tickets into your own categories, or generating reports that allow no variation. A brand voice the prompt can't sustain. When you've already tried instructions and examples and the answers are still inconsistent. Repetitive, high-volume tasks. A small model tuned for a specific task can replace a large general one, with lower latency and lower cost per query. Data that can't leave the company. If the model has to run on your infrastructure, tuning an open model can close the quality gap with a larger commercial model. Why you should consider an open model Fine-tuning a closed model looks more and more like renting. On May 7, 2026, OpenAI announced changes to the availability of its fine-tuning platform . According to coverage of the announcement, starting January 6, 2027, not even active customers will be able to create new training jobs . On top of that, fine-tuned models are retired along with their base model: GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1 mini leave the API on January 14, 2027, fine-tuned versions included . Google and AWS still offer managed fine-tuning. But each has its own pricing model, which you'll see in the cost section, and ties you to that provider's models and retirement schedules. With an open-weight model (Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma, DeepSeek), the result of the training is yours. You deploy it in your cloud, decide when to update it, and nobody shuts it off for you. You don't need a data center either. Techniques like LoRA and QLoRA train only a small additional layer on top of the model. The QLoRA authors fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU, compared with the more than 780 GB of memory that traditional 16-bit fine-tuning requires , without losing task performance. How much does RAG cost vs fine-tuning? There's no single number. Cost depends on document volume, monthly queries, the model and where it's deployed. On Google, for models from Gemini 3 onward, running a tuned model costs 1.5 times the base model price . On AWS, by contrast, customized Nova models in Bedrock can be used on demand at the same inference price as the untuned model . A worked example in relative terms Say inference with the base model costs you an amount C per month for your query volume. With Google's 1.5× surcharge, the tuned model costs 1.5C, so you pay an extra 0.5C every month. Over a year, that surcharge adds up to 6C, the equivalent of six months of base-model inference, and it keeps recurring as long as the model is in use. Add to that the training itself, which you pay once. If tuning lets you use shorter prompts, that offsets part of the surcharge. The practical takeaway: at low volume the surcharge barely matters, but at high volume it can end up costing more than the training itself. That's why you should compare it against two alternatives: RAG on the base model, which sends more text per query, or an open model in your own cloud, where you pay for servers instead of a surcharge. How to measure quality before scaling Build a set of real questions that covers your team's most common cases, each with its correct answer and the document where it lives. Use that set to check two things separately. First, retrieval: did the system find the right passage? Second, the answer: is it faithful to that passage and does it answer what was asked? If retrieval fails, the problem is in the documents or the search, not the model. Rerun the evaluation every time you change something.
