# Lovable App Not Working? What Breaks Between the Demo and Production

## Overview

The Lovable apps in the most trouble were the ones that looked fine. I went through 264 public reports of Lovable apps breaking, from Reddit, Trustpilot, GitHub and Hacker News, and sorted each one by what caused it. The biggest groups were logins and database connections (37), Lovable itself being down (33), and apps that couldn't be found on Google (32). Right behind them were 31 reports about data that was exposed or at risk, which is the one problem a working app can hide completely. I've shipped that kind of bug myself. In one of my own products, any logged-in user could see other customers' private data, because the app checked that you were logged in, but not that the data you asked for was yours. Nothing crashed and nobody complained, which is exactly why nobody had noticed. That's the real gap between a demo and production. A demo only has to work for you, on your laptop, while you watch. Production has to work for every user, on every network, when Lovable has a bad day, when your credits run out, and when someone tries to open data that isn't theirs. Below are the causes in the order I'd check them, each with the fix from Lovable's own documentation. I build on Supabase and Stripe myself, the same backend most Lovable apps run on, so I've added what I check on my own apps. At the end is what I found when I ran 53 live Lovable apps through my free checkers. What 264 broken Lovable apps had in common The reports run from 2025 to October 2026: 195 Reddit threads, 22 Trustpilot reviews, and the rest from Lovable's status page, GitHub issues, Hacker News, YouTube and security write-ups. I put each one in a group based on the cause the thread confirmed or, when nobody found one, the main symptom. Data exposure sits near the top, and it is the one cause you cannot see from inside the app. No single cause dominates, which is why "just ask the AI to fix it" fails so often. The top four groups make up about half of all reports, and the AI can't see most of them from inside the editor: an outage, a login setting in Supabase, how Google reads your site, or what a stranger can see in your database. Step 1: check Lovable, not your app When someone reports a published site is down, Lovable's own hosting docs say to check the status page first, and I agree. Open status.lovable.dev before you change anything. It tracks published sites (under Hosting) separately from the editor, so you can tell whether the problem is your app or Lovable. Its public history only covers the last 90 days, and in that window it lists 36 incidents, including previews and publishing failing on August 10, 2026 and published TanStack apps going down on August 4. The expensive mistake is spending credits during an outage. At least four reports lost credits that way, on prompts and builds that failed because Lovable itself was having problems. And if the app fails for one person but not for others, try their phone on mobile data. Two reports turned out to be an internet provider blocking the app. It works in the preview but not on the live site The preview is your working copy. The live site is a snapshot from the last time you published. Lovable's publish docs are blunt about it: edits aren't live until you open the Publish dialog and click Publish changes, and a small dot on the Publish button means you have unpublished changes. Publishing from that dialog costs no credits, even at a zero balance. If the dialog shows "Build errors need fixing" instead, publishing is blocked until the build passes. Sign-in works in the preview, fails on the live site This is the one I'd check first. Lovable's login docs say sign-in that works in the preview but fails on the published app or a custom domain is usually caused by missing Site URL and redirect URLs. On Lovable Cloud they're updated automatically when you publish. On your own Supabase project they aren't, so you have to add your live address yourself. The preview and the live app share one database This surprises people. In Lovable, the preview and the published app use the same backend and the same data, so test records you create in the preview land in your real database. Separate test and live databases were tried in a beta from February 5, 2026, then withdrawn for new Cloud projects on March 24, 2026. Login and database problems Logins and database connections were the biggest group, with 37 reports: Google sign-in breaking, password resets failing, confirmation emails that never arrived, and projects that couldn't connect to Supabase. These are the usual causes: Confirmation emails stop arriving. Lovable's login emails have an hourly sending limit, which is the first thing to check when many people sign up at once, and the default sender often lands in spam. Google sign-in shows Lovable's name. With Lovable Cloud's built-in Google login, the sign-in screen can say Lovable instead of your app. The fix one thread landed on was setting up your own Google OAuth client and consent screen. Auto-confirm is still on. Turning off email confirmation makes testing easier, but Lovable warns it lets anyone sign up with an address they don't own. Turn it back on before launch. The Supabase project is paused. A paused or still-provisioning Supabase project can't be connected to Lovable, according to its Supabase docs . The failure that does not look broken: your data is open Most Lovable apps keep their data in a Supabase database. Each table has access rules, called row level security (RLS), that decide who can read or change which rows. If a rule is missing or too open, anyone who opens your app can read data that isn't theirs, and everything still looks normal to you. Lovable says this plainly. Its security guide calls misconfigured RLS the most common cause of data exposure in Supabase-backed apps, and its Supabase docs tell you to check the rules yourself before going live. It does scan for it: a quick security scan runs on every publish and looks for tables without RLS and rules that let everyone through. But findings don't block publishing unless a workspace admin turns that on, the deeper code review is something you start by hand, and Lovable itself says its scans can't guarantee security. One thread is worth knowing about: users could see each other's data even though RLS showed as enabled, because database functions, views and storage rules can get around it. A green check on one table isn't proof for the whole app. The check I trust is the one I use on my own apps. When I built Client Approvals , I wrote a test that checks one workspace can never see another workspace's requests. You can do the manual version in a few minutes: create two normal accounts, add something private in each, then try to open one account's records while signed in as the other. Both attempts must fail. It worked yesterday, and an AI edit broke it Twenty-one reports describe the same loop: a small fix breaks two working things, and undoing it costs four or five more messages. Lovable's own debugging advice is to use Try to fix once or twice, describe the bug precisely, switch to Plan mode to find the cause, and revert if the fixes have tangled the code. Plan mode costs one credit a message and doesn't change code. Every change creates a version you can restore from the History panel . But reverting restores code only: it doesn't roll back your database or its migrations, so check your data after a revert. My habit is to stop prompting after the second failed fix and read the actual error instead. For a blank published site, Lovable says to paste the browser console error into the chat, because the agent can't see errors outside the preview. Credits ran out, and the backend went to sleep Credits are the production surprise nobody plans for. Lovable's credits page says that when credits run out, building stops, AI features in your live app fail, and the Cloud database, storage and logins pause shortly after. The pages stay up, but anything that needs the backend stops. Adding credits may not restart it: you have to click Wake up under More, Cloud, Overview. Since June 18, 2026, Lovable splits spending into Build credits, for prompting, and Run credits, for hosting and the backend of the live app. That's when people started reporting idle projects draining credits. In one thread, a background job sending email every five seconds kept a near-idle project busy. I learned this on one of my own products: its AI features had no spending limit, so one bad day could cost any amount. I added a hard cap and a switch to turn the feature off. On Lovable, the equivalent is the usage limits and alerts added on October 7, 2026. Set them before you launch, not after the first surprise. It broke when you moved it off Lovable Twenty-three reports were apps that worked on Lovable and broke on Vercel, Netlify or Cloudflare. Most trace back to one change: since May 13, 2026, new Lovable apps are built with TanStack Start, which renders pages on a server. Lovable's self-hosting guide says these apps need a host that runs server code. That's why so many exported apps built fine and then returned 404 on every page. Newer apps (TanStack Start): deploy to a host that runs server code, and check which host the build targets. In one report the build defaulted to Cloudflare, and switching it fixed Vercel. Older apps (React and Vite): add a rewrite that sends every path to index.html, or direct links and refreshes return 404. Settings: set VITE_SUPABASE_URL and VITE_SUPABASE_PUBLISHABLE_KEY on the new host before you build, and add the new domain to your login redirect URLs. What you take with you: exports include your code and database migrations, but not your data or stored files, and built-in AI calls keep running through Lovable's credits until you replace them with your own accounts. Not on Google? What I found on 53 live Lovable apps Thirty-two reports were about search: apps that never showed up on Google, or shared links with the wrong title and picture. Lovable explains the cause on its own blog : apps built before May 2026 send a nearly empty page and draw everything with JavaScript, and link previews are read from that raw page. Lovable now pre-renders those older pages for Google and other verified crawlers, and since July 28, 2026 you can upgrade an older project to server rendering in place, typically for 10 to 35 credits. It also has a free SEO and AI search review that flags a missing sitemap, robots.txt and metadata. I wanted to see what that looks like on real apps, so I took 53 live Lovable apps from Lovable's own pages, Product Hunt, Hacker News and Reddit launch posts, and ran them through my free sitemap checker , Open Graph checker and security headers checker . I only read what any browser receives. Twenty-one apps give Google no list of their pages, and nine still share Lovable's default image. Twenty-one of the 53 had no sitemap at any of the usual addresses, so Google gets no list of their pages. Nine still shared Lovable's default image, so a link to the app shows Lovable's branding instead of its own. Four pointed their share image at a file that doesn't load, and 13 used images over 300 KB, which WhatsApp often skips. Two sitemaps listed pages on addresses that don't respond, one of them a custom domain that no longer loads while the app's lovable.app address still works. Lovable's domain docs list the usual causes: a leftover AAAA record, records left over from the previous host, or Cloudflare proxying the domain (set those records to DNS only). On security, all 53 were on HTTPS, but 51 sent no Content-Security-Policy and 50 could be loaded inside another site's frame. If you host the app yourself, my security headers checker shows which headers to add. What actually changes between a demo and production Every cause in this post has the same shape. The demo worked because it only had to work once, for you. Production has to keep working for strangers, with an empty credit balance, on a bad day for Lovable, and when someone tries data that isn't theirs. I've been caught by a version of this too. One of my own products ran a daily job that published broken content about nine days a month, and nobody was told. It never threw an error. What fixed it wasn't a smarter job: it was checking the result before publishing, and sending an alert when it failed. So before you launch: publish and open the live address in a private window, sign up as a brand-new user, try to open another user's data, set credit limits and alerts, and check what your link looks like when it's shared.
