# Three Philippine Agencies Shipped AI Education Rules in One School Year. The Proof Is Still Being Assembled.

## Overview

CHED's Memorandum Order No. 21 set a national framework for AI in higher education covering 2,366 institutions (Source: CHED, 2026). Within roughly one school year, DepEd launched the country's first nationwide AI program for basic education, and TESDA opened free AI courses. What has not arrived is the measurement layer that would show whether any of it changed how Filipino students actually learn. The policy layer landed first CHED's order permits AI in teaching, learning, research and institutional operations across 2,366 higher education institutions (Source: CHED, 2026). The safeguards are explicit: academic integrity, human judgment, data privacy and student welfare. Institutions keep room to apply the framework locally, on the condition that AI strengthens human thinking rather than replaces it. That condition will be the hardest to audit. "Strengthens rather than replaces" is not a metric any HEI reports, and no national baseline exists for it. A rule can be written in a semester. Proving it changed behavior takes years. Basic education got a program and a target DepEd launched AGAP.AI (Accelerating Governance and Adaptive Pedagogy through Artificial Intelligence) on January 9, 2026, described as the Philippines' first nationwide program for responsible AI integration in basic education (Source: DepEd and Microsoft, 2026). It targets AI literacy and ethical use training for 1.5 million students, teachers and parents. The DepEd system it operates inside covers more than 24 million students. Here is the ratio: 1.5 million people targeted for training against more than 24 million students, roughly one in sixteen. DepEd Secretary Sonny Angara named the reason: "Literacy remains one of our biggest national challenges, with many learners still not meeting expected proficiency levels" (Source: DepEd and Microsoft, 2026). What has actually been measured so far The clearest numbers come from ARAL, the Academic Recovery and Accessible Learning program launched in September 2025 (Source: DepEd and Microsoft, 2026). Under it, Microsoft has begun enabling educators in 25 school districts and targets 3,000 teachers across 1,500 schools throughout 2026. Early literacy data is promising but local. Microsoft Reading Progress has assessed over 14,000 learners in 61 schools in Bais and Dumaguete, and in Cabanatuan City, 100% of learners in 3 school districts progressed to higher literacy levels (Source: DepEd and Microsoft, 2026). Sixty-one schools is a good start and a small sample. A national AI literacy figure needs the whole school system as the evaluation surface. The open channel: free skills, no gatekeeping TESDA partnered with UNESCO-UNEVOC and the HP Foundation to place HP LIFE courses on the TESDA Online Program, the national e-learning platform (Source: UNESCO, 2026). The catalog covers AI concepts, responsible AI use, entrepreneurship, marketing and finance. Every course is free and delivered online, so access depends on connectivity rather than enrollment paperwork. The ambition behind the channel is larger than one country. The UNESCO Global Skills Academy, which carries those courses, has committed to supporting ten million youth and adults globally in building essential skills by 2029 (Source: UNESCO, 2026). The market number is the least interesting number in this set Philippine edtech grew from USD 1.1 billion in 2025 to USD 1.3 billion in 2026, and is projected to reach USD 2.9 billion by 2034 at a compound annual growth rate of 10.87% (Source: IMARC Group, 2026). IMARC Group cites demand for digital learning platforms, career-focused tech training, investment in AI and Web3 education, and policy-driven digital tools among the drivers (Source: IMARC Group, 2026). Note the last one. Vendors are pricing the memorandum orders as demand, and when policy moves ahead of measurement, forecasts move faster than evidence. For providers the arithmetic runs in reverse. A company like Yano.AI, which builds AI-assisted learning products for Philippine schools and enterprises, does not sell against a market estimate. It sells against one classroom's adoption rate at a time, and the only honest proof is usage data from the schools themselves. What a credible measurement layer needs None of this requires new legislation, only budget lines and reporting discipline the agencies now have the mandate to create. A competency baseline across the full base of more than 24 million students, not a sample of 61 schools. Reporting from each of the 2,366 HEIs on how many courses use AI, and what happened to academic integrity cases afterward. Teacher-side instrumentation: how many of the 3,000 teachers reached through ARAL changed classroom practice, and whether it survived past the pilot window. Key Takeaway The Philippines shipped AI rules and programs from three agencies within one school year, across 2,366 higher education institutions, more than 24 million basic education students, and an open channel of free courses (Source: CHED, 2026; Source: DepEd and Microsoft, 2026; Source: UNESCO, 2026). The one in sixteen coverage ratio and the 61 schools of assessment data are not reasons to slow down. They are a spec for what to build next, and most of it needs no new law. So the question for every school, division and vendor is simple: if your AI contribution never appears in a national or regional dataset, can you still claim it worked? What would it take for your classrooms to be counted in the next AI literacy baseline?
