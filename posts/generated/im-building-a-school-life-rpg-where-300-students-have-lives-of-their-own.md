# I'm Building a School-Life RPG Where 300 Students Have Lives of Their Own

## Overview

I've been working on a personal project called The Merit Academy , a single-player, anime-inspired school-life RPG where you enrol at an elite academy, attend classes, take exams, build relationships, and try to make something of yourself over the course of your school life. It's heavily inspired by the kind of social strategy and academic competition you see in Classroom of the Elite , but I'm building it as its own game, with its own world and identity. The project is still early in development, but I wanted to start sharing what I'm working on, why I'm building it, and some of the challenges involved. The idea I've always liked games where the world feels like it exists independently of the player. That's the main thing I'm trying to achieve with The Merit Academy. Imagine walking through campus on a Monday morning. Some students are heading to class, others are studying in the library, and a few are probably skipping lessons entirely. You might run into someone you spoke to yesterday, but they aren't standing in the same spot waiting for you to interact with them. They have their own schedules, interests, relationships, and things to do. That's the experience I'm trying to build. A campus that changes with time One of the things I've been working on is the academy itself. The campus includes academic buildings, student facilities, sports areas, and places where students can spend their free time. I also wanted the environment to change throughout the year. Spring brings cherry blossoms, autumn changes the colours of the campus, winter covers everything in snow, and rainy days give the whole place a different atmosphere. It's a relatively small detail compared to the gameplay systems, but I think it makes a huge difference when you're spending dozens of hours in the same world. I'm working toward a system that can support hundreds of students without making the campus feel overcrowded or turning every NPC into someone who just stands around waiting for the player. The idea is that students have routines based on their personalities, interests, and responsibilities. Someone who's serious about academics might spend more time in the library. An athlete might head to practice after class. Others might hang out with friends or visit different campus facilities. Not everyone needs to be visible on the map at the same time. And if you arrange to meet someone somewhere, their schedule should account for that. I'm still developing and refining these systems, but getting this right is one of my biggest priorities. School should actually feel like school Another thing I wanted to avoid was making lessons and exams feel like buttons you click to increase a stat. I want studying to mean something. When you attend a lesson, you should have opportunities to learn, answer questions, and prepare for future exams. If you tutor another student, you should actually help them understand the material rather than simply clicking a "Tutor" button and watching their academic ability increase. The same applies to relationships. Conversations shouldn't just be a way to collect relationship points. Your answers, the context, and the personality of the person you're speaking to should all matter. The goal is to make the different systems feel connected rather than like separate minigames. The technical challenge I'm a software developer by profession, but building a game with interconnected simulation systems is a different kind of challenge. There are quite a few things I'm working through: Simulating student schedules without unnecessarily rendering hundreds of characters. Keeping track of relationships, reputation, and decisions over time. Making academic progression meaningful without turning it into a repetitive grind. Connecting NPC behaviour with story events and player interactions. Keeping the game responsive as more systems and characters are introduced. One of the things I've learned is that adding a feature is often easier than making it interact naturally with everything else. A simple decision like meeting another student after class can affect schedules, relationships, dialogue, locations, and potentially future events. That's where things get interesting. Where the project stands The Merit Academy is being developed independently, and there's no release date yet. I'm still building and improving the foundations, experimenting with gameplay systems, and figuring out what works. Some features are already implemented, some are being reworked, and others are still ideas I want to explore. I'm trying to be transparent about that rather than presenting every planned feature as something that's already playable. Following the development I've started documenting the project publicly, including the things that work, the things that don't, and what I'm planning next.
