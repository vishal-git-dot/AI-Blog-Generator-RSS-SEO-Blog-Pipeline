# How We Update Client Websites Without Breaking the Live Site

## Overview

Website updates break sites in boring, predictable ways: a plugin conflicts with the theme, a deploy overwrites a config, a form silently stops submitting. The fix is not better luck. It is a repeatable process: staging, backup, deploy, verify, rollback plan. Here is the exact process we run at aimEncoders on every client update, and the checks we run after each deploy. Why a Small Update Can Cause a Big Problem Most updates are routine. A plugin releases a new version, WordPress pushes a security fix, a theme gets a compatibility patch. The button says "update now", and it looks like a one-click job. The risk is what sits behind that button. Your site is made of pieces built by different people at different times: the core platform, the theme, a handful of plugins, custom code, and third-party tools for payments, bookings, or chat. An update to one piece can disagree with another. The site still loads, the homepage still looks right, and the problem hides on a page nobody checked. A customer finds it first. That is why we never treat an update as a simple click. The work that keeps a site safe happens before and after the update itself, not during it. How We Update a Client Website, Step by Step 1. Test the change on a staging copy first A staging site is a private copy of the live website. It looks and behaves the same way, but visitors never see it. We apply the update there first, so any problem appears in a safe place instead of in front of customers. Say a client wants a new version of their booking plugin. On staging, we install it and walk through the whole booking flow: pick a date, choose a time, submit the form, check the confirmation. If the date picker breaks or a step goes blank, we find out here and fix it here, and the live site never feels a thing. Only when staging behaves do we move forward. We run the same kind of checks on staging that we run before a launch, including our Playwright checks on the key pages and forms. 2. Take a full backup before touching anything Before we change the live site, we take a complete backup: the files, the database, the images, and the settings. WordPress itself recommends backing up before updates, and we follow that advice on every site we manage, not just the big ones. You can read the official guidance in the WordPress updating documentation ( https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/updating-wordpress/ ). A backup only helps if it is fresh and complete. A copy from three months ago will not bring back last week's enquiries or yesterday's orders. So we take the backup right before the update, and we store it away from the live site, so a problem with the hosting account does not take the backup down with it. This step takes minutes. Recovering a site without a backup can take days. 3. Deploy the change, then check the parts that earn money Once staging passes and the backup is in place, we apply the update to the live site. Then the real work starts: checking that the site still does its job. We do not just load the homepage and call it done. The homepage can look perfect while the contact form quietly fails. We open the pages visitors use most, submit a test enquiry through the form, and check that it arrives. On a store, we walk through the cart and checkout. On a booking site, we test a booking end to end. We check the menus, the buttons, and the pages on a real phone, because that is how most Pakistani customers will see the site. This is the step that catches the problems nobody expects. A plugin update once changed how a form handled phone numbers, and the form looked fine until someone actually tried to submit it. The homepage check would have missed it. The form check caught it in minutes. 4. Keep a rollback plan ready Even with staging and backups, a live server can surprise you. A plugin can behave differently on the live server than it did on staging. A third-party service can stop responding at exactly the wrong moment. So before every significant update, we decide in advance how we would undo it. Sometimes rolling back means reversing one plugin to its previous version. Sometimes it means restoring files from the backup we just took. Either way, the decision is made before the update, not in a panic after something breaks. We also think about what changed since the backup: restoring an old database on a busy store could wipe out new orders, so the recovery has to be planned around the site's real activity, not just its files. 5. Pick a quiet hour Timing matters. Updating a store in the middle of its busiest afternoon is asking for trouble, even with everything else done right. So we look at when the site is actually quiet and schedule bigger updates for then. For most Pakistani businesses, that means late at night or early in the morning, when traffic is low and few customers are browsing. The right hour is different for every site, so we check the site's own analytics instead of guessing. A site serving customers overseas may have its quiet hours at a completely different time of day. Small security patches do not always need a maintenance window, but anything that touches the theme, the checkout, or a booking flow gets a quiet slot and enough time to test properly. 6. Tell the client what changed Most business owners do not want a technical report. They want to know three things: what changed, whether anything will look different, and whether the site is ready for customers again. So that is what we tell them, in plain words, before and after the work. Before a bigger update, we explain what is being updated and why, and whether visitors will notice anything. If a design change is involved, we share a preview from staging so the client can approve it before it goes live. After the update, we confirm what was done and what we checked. If something took longer than expected, we say so, and we say why. A client who knows what is happening trusts the process. The Final Check Before We Call It Done Every update ends with the same short list, and we do not call the work finished until it passes. We open the homepage and the most important service and product pages. We submit the contact form and confirm the message arrives. We click through the menus and check the links. We look at the site on a phone and a tablet. We check images, buttons, and anything interactive. On a store, we test the cart and checkout with a test payment, never a real order. We watch for error messages and slow pages. The list is short on purpose. It covers the things a customer would hit first, and it takes minutes. When an Update Still Goes Wrong Sometimes it still goes wrong. A plugin update can reveal a conflict that staging did not show, or a third-party tool can change something on its side overnight. Pretending that never happens would be dishonest. What matters is the response. First, we stop and find out what broke. We do not pile more changes on top of a problem we do not understand yet. Then we judge the impact: a spacing issue on a secondary page is one thing, a checkout that will not take payments is another. If the problem is serious, we roll back to the version that worked, using the backup and the plan we made before the update. Only once the site is stable do we investigate the cause, test a fix on staging, and try again. The client hears about it the same day, in plain language: what broke, what we did about it, and what happens next. Why This Process Matters for Your Business A careful update process buys you four things. It keeps customers moving: a form that works and a checkout that completes mean enquiries and orders keep coming in while the site improves in the background. It protects your content and your data: backups and staging mean years of pages, posts, and customer records are never one bad click away from disappearing. It protects your search visibility: a broken page or a sudden error can push a site down in Google. And it gives you confidence: when you know updates are handled carefully, you stop worrying about the website and get back to running the business. Your website will need updates for as long as it exists. Security patches, new features, better design, faster pages. The question is never whether to update. It is whether the updates happen carefully or carelessly. We choose carefully, every time. Frequently Asked Questions Can updating a website break it? Yes. An update can clash with a plugin, a theme, or a third-party tool, and the problem may only show on one page or one device. Testing on staging, taking a backup, and checking the site after the update are how you keep that risk small. Should website updates be tested before they go live? For anything beyond a tiny patch, yes. A staging copy lets you test the update without touching the live site. If something breaks, you fix it in private and your customers never see the problem. What is a staging website? It is a private copy of your live site used for testing. It looks and works the same way, but only your team can see it. Changes are tried there first, then moved to the live site once they pass. Do I need a backup before updating my website? Yes, always take a fresh one before a significant update. It should cover the files and the database, and it should be stored away from the live site. If an update goes badly, that backup is your way back. Can website updates be done without downtime? Most can. Small updates usually finish with no interruption at all. Bigger changes may need a short maintenance window, which is why we schedule them during quiet hours and keep the work as quick as possible. How often should a business website be updated? Ongoing, not once a year. Security patches should go out promptly, plugins and themes should be reviewed regularly, and backups and checks should run on a schedule. A site with daily orders or bookings needs more frequent attention than a simple brochure site.
