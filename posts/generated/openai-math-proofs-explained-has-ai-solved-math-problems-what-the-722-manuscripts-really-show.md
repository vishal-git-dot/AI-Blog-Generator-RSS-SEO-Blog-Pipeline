# OpenAI Math Proofs Explained: Has AI Solved Math Problems? What the 722 Manuscripts Really Show

## Overview

On 6 October 2026, OpenAI posted 722 maths manuscripts from an unreleased AI model to GitHub, claiming progress on 372 open problems. A day later it withdrew three over a sign error. So has AI solved math problems that humans couldn't? Some, very probably. Most, nobody knows yet. That gap between "claimed" and "checked" is the real story behind the OpenAI math proofs, and the business lesson: AI made answers cheap, and checking them is now the bottleneck. OpenAI's unreleased model produced 722 manuscripts claiming progress on 372 open problems, and leading number theorists largely agree its quasi-Riemann result is correct. But only about 42% of the top-line results have machine-checked Lean proofs, three papers were withdrawn the next day, and as of 26 October 2026 most of the rest still await expert review. Impressive: the quasi-Riemann result, which Stanford's Jared Duker Lichtman calls the most significant AI maths achievement so far. Unverified: the results without Lean proofs are long English write-ups that experts have not finished reading. Already corrected: three manuscripts withdrawn and 14 revised within 24 hours of release. Not solved: the Riemann hypothesis itself. The Navier-Stokes claim from September is still being checked, and the Clay Mathematics Institute has not ruled on it. The quasi-Riemann hypothesis in plain English The Riemann hypothesis is arguably the most famous open problem in mathematics. It's about the zeta function, whose "zeros" (the inputs where it equals zero) control how prime numbers are spread out. Riemann showed that the interesting zeros sit inside a vertical strip between 0 and 1; the hypothesis says they all sit exactly on the line down the middle, at 1/2. In 1896, two mathematicians showed that no zeros sit on the strip's edges, which was enough to prove the prime number theorem. The quasi-Riemann hypothesis asks for less than the full hypothesis: just prove all the zeros live in some narrower strip. As Quanta puts it, "the narrower the strip, the more one can say about how the primes are distributed." OpenAI released two versions: The 7/8 version (dated 30 September): all the zeros lie between 1/8 and 7/8. It's 199 pages and was formally verified in Lean. The 11/12 version (about a week later): a wider strip, between 1/12 and 11/12, in 49 pages. It's not yet verified in Lean. It's the only one of the released papers a human edited, and OpenAI hasn't said who. Mathematicians mostly read the shorter one. NYU's Maksym Radziwiłł called the implications "staggering", noting that "the vast majority of consequences of RH already follow from quasi-RH". Stanford's Jared Duker Lichtman called it "the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far, by quite a margin". Georgia Tech's Alexander Dunn called it "a surprising synthesis of well-known ideas"; it relies in part on techniques Radziwiłł and Dunn pioneered. And the caution: Princeton's Peter Sarnak won't call it "a Theorem" until humans check every detail. Several expert write-ups exist but haven't been published. How maths gets verified: peer review vs proof checkers like Lean There are two ways a proof becomes trusted. Peer review. Experts read the argument line by line, often for months, then a journal publishes it. It depends on human attention, which is why Radziwiłł's verdict that the papers "are horribly written (in a way that would lead to immediate rejection at any mathematical journal)" matters. Formal proof checking. Lean is a programming language and proof assistant. You write the theorem and every step of the proof in code, and Lean's small trusted "kernel" checks that each step follows from the last. If it compiles, the logic holds, with no reviewer's patience required. Lean has one big catch: it checks the theorem as written in Lean , not the English paper. If the AI translates the question wrongly, Lean will happily confirm a proof of something slightly different. On 6 October, three researchers (Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen) posted a paper arguing exactly this about OpenAI's Navier-Stokes formalisation: that the Lean proof doesn't match the natural-language argument. So the strongest evidence is both: a Lean proof whose statement an expert has confirmed matches the real problem, plus a human-readable paper. Which OpenAI math proofs are verified? The lesson for businesses: generation is cheap, verification is the bottleneck OpenAI's model averaged about three hours per result. Checking the best one has taken the world's leading number theorists weeks, and they're not finished. Your AI won't be proving theorems, but the shape is the same. A model drafts 200 contract summaries or a pull request in minutes; a person still has to decide which are right. If review capacity doesn't grow with output, you haven't done more work. You have a bigger pile of unchecked drafts. Three patterns from the maths story apply directly: Survivorship. You see 372 wins, not 3,600-odd failures. If you only log the AI outputs that worked, you'll overestimate how reliable it is. Cascading errors. One sign error broke three papers. One wrong figure in an AI-written report can flow into a forecast, a quote and an invoice. "Verified" can verify the wrong thing. A Lean proof of a mistranslated question is worthless. A test suite that passes against the wrong spec is too. How to build verification into AI workflows Define "correct" before you generate. Write the acceptance check first: the fields that must match the source, the totals that must reconcile, the tests that must pass. Make the machine check what it can. Lean is maths' version of this. In business it's schema validation, recomputing every number, resolving every citation and link, and running tests on generated code. Our vibe coding security checklist covers this for AI-written code. Check the question, not just the answer. Have a person confirm the AI understood the task, because a perfect answer to the wrong question still passes automated checks. Review by risk. Spot-check low-stakes output; require a named human sign-off on anything that touches money, customers or legal commitments. Label what's verified. Mark AI output as "checked" or "unchecked" so nobody builds on a draft by mistake. That was Townsend's point. Keep the failures. Log what the AI got wrong as well as what it got right, and measure accuracy over time. Our guide to evaluating a RAG chatbot shows how. Version and correct in the open. OpenAI kept earlier versions and a public changelog. Do the same for AI-generated documents, so a correction reaches everything that used the old version.
