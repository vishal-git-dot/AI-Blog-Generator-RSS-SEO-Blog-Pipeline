# I wanted to 3D-print a fox an AI made from one picture. The fix was to stop looking for its surface

## Overview

Image-to-3D models are good now. You give Idol one picture of a toy fox, and about a minute later you can spin a 3D fox in your browser, including the back it never saw. Then you try to print it, and you hit a wall: the model did not make a mesh. It made a Gaussian splat , a cloud of soft, semi-transparent blobs. Slicers want a closed surface, and a cloud of blobs has none. This post is about getting from that cloud to a clean, watertight STL. The method that worked is old and simple. Getting to it took four failed attempts, and the failures are the useful part. Code: https://github.com/helgard-orlm/splat2stl . It converts a splat in 8–16 seconds on an 8 GB RTX 5060 with one command. Who did what, up front: I set the goal, judged every result in a slicer, and made the two calls that mattered: "your first version was almost perfect, everything after it is worse" and "why not photograph the fox from all sides, like photogrammetry apps do". The code and the experiments were done by Claude (Anthropic) in my sessions. GLM-5.3 acted as a second opinion: it found the wrong assumption behind the failed attempts and reviewed the README before release. "We" below means the three of us. What a splat is, in one paragraph A splat stores a few hundred thousand ellipsoid blobs. Each blob has a position, a size, a rotation, a color and an opacity. A viewer draws them front to back and blends them, the way layers of tinted glass add up. There is no lighting step: the colors already contain the shading, including any glossy highlights that were in the input picture. The obvious approaches, and why they failed Every textbook route from points to a surface has the same shape: decide where the surface is, then wrap triangles around it. So we tried to find the surface. Threshold the blobs. Treat every blob above some opacity as solid and wrap it. The fox came out with bumps on its head and face that you could not see in the browser. Find the depth where the fox becomes opaque. From 60 directions, march through the blobs and mark the point where accumulated opacity crosses 50%. The head came out pitted, "as if eaten by acid", in my words at the time. Average depth plus Poisson reconstruction. Take the viewer's average depth from many directions as a point cloud, then let screened Poisson (the algorithm behind MeshLab's reconstruction filter) stretch a smooth skin over it. The head came out grainy. Blur the opacity and take one iso-surface. Smooth by construction, or so we thought. The face came out swollen and covered in bubbles. The body and the tail were fine in every attempt. Only the head kept failing. Halfway through, we finally printed some statistics about the head, and they already held the answer. It took two more attempts and a second opinion to accept it. The head is fog On the body, the median blob opacity is 0.85 , and 67% of the blobs are more than half opaque. On the head, the median opacity is 0.01 , and only 4% are more than half opaque. Idol built the head out of tens of thousands of almost transparent blobs stacked in layers. In the viewer, these layers add up to a smooth, solid-looking head. Across the whole fox, 51% of the blobs have opacity below 0.1. We generated the same fox with three other random seeds and got 47%, 51% and 54%, so this is how the model sees this picture, not bad luck. A stone house or a pirate ship from the same model was only 3–7% fog, which is why the methods worked on them. That explained all four failures at once, and GLM put it bluntly: inside fog there is no surface to find. Where the fog "becomes solid" depends on the direction you look from. Every method that searches for that place turns the disagreement between directions into artifacts: bumps, pits, grain or bubbles. The four attempts were not four bugs. They were one wrong assumption. Stop looking for the surface. Use the outline Here is the thing that is well defined even for fog: the outline. Look at the fox from any direction, and the opacity accumulated through the whole head is high, so its silhouette is crisp. My first version had already used silhouettes, which is why it was "almost perfect". Its glitches came from building those silhouettes out of individual blobs with a low threshold, so stray wisps of fog poked out as small bumps. The fix was to take the outline from real pictures of the splat, exactly as the browser shows it: Photograph it. We wrote a small splat renderer in plain PyTorch (no CUDA extension to compile). It draws each blob as a soft ellipse and blends the layers front to back, like the browser does. It takes about 140 pictures from directions spread over a sphere, from the top down to 60° below the horizon. Take the outline in each picture : every pixel where the accumulated opacity is above 50%. Carve like a sculptor. Start with a block of 256³ voxels. A voxel survives only if it falls inside the outline in every picture. What remains looks like the fox from every direction, and it is solid inside. Skin and color. Marching cubes turns the voxels into triangles, light smoothing removes the voxel steps, and each vertex takes its color from the picture that sees it most head-on. This is the classic visual hull , known since the 1990s ( Laurentini, 1994 ). Nothing here is a new algorithm. What is specific is where the outline comes from: from properly blended renders of the splat, not from the blobs themselves. That is the difference between fog becoming bumps and fog becoming a smooth head. We also tried real photogrammetry on those pictures (COLMAP's patch-match stereo with the known cameras). Its depth was accurate where it found texture, about 0.35 mm at a print height of 80 mm, but it found texture on only about half of the fox. Using it to carve concavities roughened the smooth plastic, so it is not part of the tool. Its real value was proving that clean pictures were the key. Results We tested twelve objects: eleven generated by Idol and one pirate ship generated by TripoSplat, another image-to-3D model. All twelve meshes are watertight, and each took 8–16 seconds. The converter needs about 2.4 GB of GPU memory. The limitations are the classic ones for this method, and you can see them in the picture: Hidden hollows are filled. If no direction can see into a hollow, the outline cannot carve it. The seat of the bowl-shaped papasan chair is filled flat, and so is the inside of the watering can. Handles and arches survive, because you can see through them from the side. Very thin parts disappear. Anything thinner than about two voxels (≈0.6 mm on an 80 mm print) is lost to smoothing: ship rigging, the thin rattan stand of the papasan chair. Highlights are baked into the colors. The fox was glossy in the input picture, so the colored OBJ has light patches on its head. The STL geometry is unaffected. The clean fix is to make the input picture matte before generating. Try it git clone https://github.com/helgard-orlm/splat2stl && cd splat2stl pip install -e . python -m splat2stl fox.ply out/fox --height 80 # out/fox.stl (Z up, millimetres) and out/fox_color.obj (vertex colors) It expects a splat of a single object. --up sets the up axis ( -y by default, which matches Idol, TripoSplat and most 3DGS generators). --smooth (0–5) trades detail for smoothness. What I took away Look at the data before tuning the method, and believe it. Two variants of "find the depth" went by before anyone printed the opacity statistics of the head. Even after the numbers said "fog", we spent two more attempts searching that fog for a surface. One table explained everything; it just had to be taken seriously. When the first version is "almost right", fix its specific glitches. Each new attempt replaced the whole approach and threw away what was working. The final tool is version one with its silhouettes taken from proper renders. Measure what is well defined, not what you wish you had. Depth inside fog does not exist. The outline does. Building on the outline gave a solid that is closed by construction, which is exactly what a printer needs. Credits: 3D Gaussian Splatting by Kerbl et al. They recover hollows on clean, opaque splats, which a silhouette method cannot do.
