# An 84% Win Rate and Still Losing Money: Why I Built a Public Record for Stock Predictions

## Overview

It started with failing, repeatedly A friend introduced me to prop trading firms — pass their evaluation, trade their capital. I failed the tests. Not once: many times, and always the same way. Emotional overtrading. I knew the rules; I broke them anyway. The problem wasn't my analysis. It was my hands. So I did what a developer does when the problem is human: I tried to remove the human. I would build a trading bot. Seven months of humility I expected the hard part to be code. It wasn't. Over seven months I tried strategy after strategy before converging on something specific: small-cap stocks, a holding period of up to five days, a 5.5% profit target, a 25% stop loss. I funded a live account with $500 — a constraint that is itself a challenge. Under the pattern day trader rule, accounts under $25,000 are limited to three day trades a week, so I pivoted to a cash account and built the system around settlement mechanics instead of fighting them. And then the market taught me the lesson this whole article is about. The math that humbles you Look at that configuration again: win 5.5%, lose 25%. Each loss erases about four and a half wins. Run the arithmetic and the breakeven win rate is 25 ÷ (25 + 5.5) ≈ 82% . My live account's win rate today is 83.9% — 99 winners out of 118 resolved trades. By most people's intuition, an 84% win rate is a money machine. Mine is a coin toss with extra steps: 83.9% against a breakeven of 82% is a sliver of edge that fees, slippage, and one bad fill can erase. The account is down $60 from where it started. An 83.9% win rate, $60 underwater. Win rate without context is a story, not a statistic. That was the insight I couldn't unlearn: win rate, alone, is close to meaningless. What matters is win rate relative to what your win/loss sizes require — and almost nobody who posts their results tells you the second number. Two more tuition payments along the way: paper and live accounts diverge on identical trades — fills you get on paper simply don't happen in a real queue — and a brief experiment with put options ended after two trades and roughly $150 in losses. Some lessons are cheap only in hindsight. The system did stabilize. From a low of $338 on May 8th, the account has climbed back to $439 over three months of live, rule-bound, emotionless trading. Not riches — discipline, finally, enforced by code. The record I wanted to read Somewhere in month six, I went looking for reputable signal providers — people whose predictions I could learn from or verify. What I found instead was an industry of screenshots. Track records that start after the losses. Winners posted, losers deleted. Ten accounts run in parallel and only the lucky one shown. There was no way to tell skill from survivorship — because nothing prevented editing the past. I couldn't find a record I trusted. So I built the thing I wanted to read. quantrank500.com — locked before the open, settled by the market, impossible to edit. QuantRank500 is a free, open-source public record of stock predictions. It works like this: You lock a prediction before the market opens. Until 9:30 AM ET, only its cryptographic fingerprint is public — the content is sealed, so nobody can claim a call after the move. At the open it's revealed and becomes a limit order. The market fills it or doesn't. Both are recorded. Settlement is automatic, against exchange trade records. Stop, target, or expiry — nobody reports their own results. Nothing can be edited or deleted. Wins, losses, and the entries the market never touched stay on the record, forever. Everyone competes with the same simulated $500 account — the same stake I started with, because that number keeps everyone honest about scale. And the scoreboard ranks by a conservative lower bound of profit factor, not win rate — because I learned the hard way what an 84% win rate is worth. A record on the sandbox. Note the second row: every profile shows the number most track records hide — the breakeven win rate its style requires, next to the actual. Survivorship is public, too: the site shows how many identities ever posted against how many survived to a ranked record. The graveyard doesn't disappear. The scoreboard: ranked by the lower bound of profit factor, with survivorship stated at the bottom — how many started vs. how many survived. No accounts, no emails, no ads, no data sales. The ledger is public domain and the code is open source. The first user is the machine that started all this There's a symmetry I enjoy: the ledger's very first record — prediction #1 — was posted by my trading bot, automatically, from the same signal engine that trades my live account. It committed a long position in Nokia the night before, sealed, revealed at the open, now waiting for the market's verdict. My real account holds the same position. One of us is on the permanent record. Three takeaways Win rate lies without its context. The only honest question is whether your win rate clears the breakeven your win/loss sizes demand. Mine clears it by 1.9 points. That gap is the entire business. Paper is not live. Simulated fills are optimistic in ways you only discover with real money in a real queue. Any record built purely on simulation should say so. Trust requires records that can't be edited. Not screenshots, not self-reports — precommitment and automatic settlement. If the past is editable, it isn't a track record; it's marketing. Take a look — and criticize it.
