# The 30% superforecaster claim doesn't survive contact with the papers

## Overview

I write research briefs, which means the thing I sell is the gap between a claim and its source. So I test myself with it: pick a statistic that travels well, trace it back, see what is left. I picked the superforecaster one because I had already repeated it. The claim "Superforecasters are 30% more accurate than intelligence analysts with access to classified information." It sounds like a receipt. It has a number, a comparison, and a secret. That is why it travels. Where it comes from The Good Judgment Project, the research team run by Philip Tetlock and Barbara Mellers, which won the IARPA forecasting tournament. The tournament was real: teams competed on live geopolitical questions and were scored on accuracy. What holds up Good Judgment won, and not narrowly. The tournament results put them ahead of the other research teams by 35 to 72%. Their plain median forecast was that accurate as well, so the win is not an artifact of a clever aggregation trick. The peer-reviewed backbone is real too: Mellers et al., "Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament," Psychological Science (2014), doi:10.1177/0956797614524255 . This is where the skills behind superforecasting get measured. Atanasov et al., "Small steps to accuracy: Incremental belief updaters are better forecasters," Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes (2020), doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.02.001 . The finding is what it sounds like: forecasters who update in small increments beat the ones who make dramatic revisions. What doesn't The 30% figure. It lives on the seller's own site, not in the papers. The papers report a 35 to 72% range against other research teams. "30% more accurate than intelligence analysts with access to classified information" is a different comparison against a different opponent, and I could not find it in the peer-reviewed record. The lead-time figure has a similar problem. Secondary write-ups give it two ways: superforecasters 400 days out matching regular forecasters at 150 days, or 300 against 100. Same underlying analysis, different arithmetic. One of those is wrong, and the people repeating it have not checked which. This is not a scandal. Nobody had to lie. A number became useful, then got repeated, and each repetition dropped the comparison that made it true. The honest version People who keep score and correct in small steps beat people who are confident and hold no receipts. That is smaller than the 30% headline, and it is the version that survives contact with the sources. If I cannot trace a claim to something you can check, it is not a finding, it is a feeling with a link on it.
