# How We Built a News App From Scratch — With No Ads, No Paid News Placement, and a Tiny Team

## Overview

How We Built a News App From Scratch — Without Ads or Paid News Placement I wanted to build a news app differently. Not another app that simply shows more headlines. Not another feed where the same story appears from ten different publications. And not another product where advertisements compete for your attention while you're trying to understand what happened. We wanted to build something simple: Give people the important information, remove the noise, and let them understand the story quickly. So we started building from scratch. No existing news-app codebase. No newsroom. No large team. No advertising network. Just a small team, APIs, AI models, servers, a lot of debugging, and plenty of things that broke along the way. That project eventually became TheReader.AI . We launched it on iOS and eventually reached the Top 60 in the News category . Here's what we learned building it. The problem wasn't a lack of news There was already no shortage of news apps. The problem was the opposite. There was too much news. If I wanted to follow a company, an industry, a market, or even a particular topic, I could easily end up opening dozens of articles every day. And very often, those articles were about the same event. One publication would report something. Another publication would report the same event from a different angle. Then another would publish an analysis of the same story. For a reader, that can create the feeling of being informed without necessarily making it easier to understand what actually happened. We started asking a different question: What if a news app focused less on giving people more articles and more on helping them understand the important ones? That became the foundation of TheReader.AI. The first challenge wasn't AI summarization When we started, we thought the difficult part would be summarization. It wasn't. Sending an article to an AI model and asking it to summarize the content is relatively straightforward. The difficult question comes before that: Which article should we summarize? A single event can generate dozens of articles. We needed to build a system that could collect news from different sources, identify similar stories, understand relevance, and decide which stories deserved attention. Our pipeline started looking something like this: News sources → Ingestion → Duplicate detection → Relevance scoring → AI summarization → Ranking → Personalized feed Each step created a different engineering problem. Duplicate news is surprisingly difficult At first, it sounds easy. Just compare the headlines. But real-world news doesn't work that way. Consider these three headlines: Company X announces a new product Company X enters a new market with its latest product What Company X's latest announcement means for investors They could all be referring to the same event. At the same time, two articles can have very similar headlines but actually describe completely different developments. So simple string matching wasn't enough. We experimented with different approaches to identify stories that were genuinely related. This became an important part of the system because we didn't want to show users five versions of essentially the same story. We wanted to show the story once, while still allowing readers to explore different sources when necessary. Then came the summarization problem Once we had identified the important stories, we had another challenge. An AI-generated summary should not simply be a shorter version of an article. It needs to preserve the information that actually matters. Numbers matter. Names matter. Dates matter. Context matters. And sometimes a single sentence in an article can completely change the meaning of the story. We therefore spent a lot of time experimenting with prompts, models, summary lengths and validation. The goal was simple: A reader should be able to understand the main development without opening five different articles. That sounds easy. Getting it consistently right is not. Ranking news is another problem Even after collecting and summarizing thousands of stories, we still had to answer another question: What should appear first? The latest story isn't always the most important story. The story with the most clicks isn't necessarily the most useful story. And a popular topic isn't necessarily relevant to every reader. We started considering multiple signals, including: Recency Relevance Story importance Source quality Similarity to other stories User interests Breaking-news signals The goal wasn't to create an endless feed. It was to create a feed where the first few stories were actually worth reading. Building the app was only half the work The mobile app was another challenge. We had to build the experience around a simple idea: Less reading. More understanding. That affected everything from the way stories were displayed to how users could follow topics and receive notifications. Behind the scenes, there was also a backend handling news ingestion, processing, AI summarization, ranking and personalization. We were constantly balancing product experience with infrastructure costs. AI APIs aren't free. News APIs aren't free. Servers aren't free. And running a news product means that the amount of data you process can grow very quickly. Why no ads? One of our decisions from the beginning was to keep the product ad-free. We didn't want the reading experience to be interrupted by advertisements. We also didn't want the news ranking to become a place where paid placement could influence what users see. That doesn't mean we have solved the business model perfectly. In fact, building an ad-free news product creates its own challenges. But we wanted to start with the product experience we believed in and figure out sustainable monetization around it. We also wanted to reduce editorial bias This is probably one of the hardest parts of building a news product. There is no perfect algorithm for deciding what is important. Every ranking system has assumptions. Every source has its own editorial perspective. Every summarization model can make mistakes. So rather than claiming that we can completely eliminate bias, our goal is to reduce unnecessary editorial influence and give readers more context . Where possible, we want readers to understand the underlying story rather than simply being told what to think about it. That's a much harder problem than it sounds. Then we launched Eventually, we had something that we were comfortable putting in front of real users. We launched TheReader.AI on iOS. And something interesting happened. People actually started using it. The app eventually reached the Top 60 in the News category on iOS . For a small team building a news product from scratch, that was a pretty exciting milestone. But the ranking itself wasn't the most valuable part. It was the validation that there was a real problem worth solving. People don't necessarily need another place to consume more information. They need better ways to understand the information they already have. What we learned Building a news app taught us that the visible product is only a small part of the problem. The hard work happens underneath. Collecting reliable information. Understanding whether two stories are actually the same. Determining which stories matter. Generating accurate summaries. Ranking information. Keeping infrastructure costs under control. And building an experience that doesn't constantly compete for the user's attention. We're still working on all of these problems. TheReader.AI is far from finished. But that's also what makes building it interesting. We're not trying to build another place where people spend more time reading news. We're trying to build a tool that helps people spend less time reading and understand more. And there is still a lot left to build.
