The Intelligence Community's Walk-Back The original bombshell, first reported by the New York Times on October 29, alleged that Jensen Huang had been the target of a Chinese espionage plot through his relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), CEO of Singapore-based cloud firm Megaspeed. The Financial Times cited a classified U.S. intelligence memo suggesting she had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. The allegation landed like a depth charge. Nvidia's stock dropped over 4% on the first trading day, erasing roughly $200 billion in market cap . The board opened an independent investigation. Huang emailed staff admitting to marital infidelity and pledging cooperation. The Hacker News discussion alone drew 490 points and 403 comments . China's foreign ministry called the allegations "groundless smears." President Trump defended Huang publicly. Sacks argued that the only established crime so far is the leak of the classified memo itself — and called for a leak investigation rather than a spy hunt. But it means the market is pricing in a spy-thriller premium on a story where the intelligence community itself cannot agree on the plot. The Money Trail That Does Not Depend on Espionage Strip the espionage question away entirely. What remains is a financial story with three independent threads, each carrying its own governance risk. Thread 1: Mark Stevens' $947 Million Exit Mark Stevens, a long-serving Nvidia board director and billionaire venture capitalist, sold approximately 4.2 million shares in Q3 2026 for roughly $947 million — the largest insider sale by any U.S. corporate director that quarter. The sales ran in three batches: $411 million across August 31 to September 2, $236 million on September 3-4, and $300 million on September 18. The critical detail: no Rule 10b5-1 trading plan was flagged in the SEC filings for the initial batches. Pre-set trading plans are the standard mechanism insiders use to demonstrate that large sales are routine and not based on material non-public information. Stevens may hold a grandfathered pre-2023 arrangement that does not require the same disclosure — but in the current environment, the optics are brutal. Including earlier June sales, his total dispositions since the start of summer approach $1.35 billion. The question governance analysts are asking: what did Stevens know, and when? The spy story broke publicly on October 29. Stevens' largest batch was September 2. The board says it had no advance knowledge. The market has to decide whether it believes that. Thread 3: Circular Financing — Nvidia's Self-Reinforcing Revenue Loop The third thread predates the scandal but converges with it. Nvidia has faced persistent questions about circular financing — arrangements where the company invests in or lends to entities that are simultaneously its largest customers. The scale is staggering. Bloomberg has documented a financing web of more than $750 billion in which Nvidia invests in chip buyers who borrow to buy more chips. Jensen Huang rejected the label, calling the suggestion "ridiculous" and noting Nvidia's strategic investments total only $4.7 billion year-to-date against massive revenues. But the critics have names and numbers. Michael Burry has compared Nvidia not to Enron but to Cisco during the dot-com bubble — arguing that hyperscalers are extending GPU useful-life assumptions from 2-3 years to 5-6 years to flatter earnings. The Financial Times calls it old-fashioned vendor financing with a new label. A Hacker News thread titled "Inside the Circular Financing of the GPU Boom" drew 370 points and 182 comments, with practitioners debating whether the revenue is real or manufactured. View discussion on Hacker News → One data point cuts through the debate: the cost of insuring Nvidia's debt against default has tripled in 2026 . As we wrote in Nvidia Is the Central Bank of AI , the company has positioned itself as the backstop of GPU debt. That was a feature when the stock was climbing. It becomes a governance vulnerability when the CEO who built the system is suddenly gone. The Succession Vacuum Naval Ravikant's post captured the bull case in five words: "Jensen Huang is the Steve Jobs of Nvidia. He is irreplaceable." 85,000 likes and 4 million views suggest the market agrees — which is precisely the governance problem. The most commonly cited backup is CFO Colette Kress, who has been EVP and CFO since 2013 and is deeply respected on Wall Street — but she has never run a product organization or led a hardware engineering team. A structural detail matters: Huang holds no super-voting stock. Unlike Zuckerberg at Meta or the Google founders, he can actually be removed by a standard board vote. The board expressed confidence in him and said he pledged full cooperation with the investigation.
