# How to Choose a New Car: A Step-by-Step Guide and What to Look For

## Overview

The choice of a car almost always follows the wrong order: first, people pick a model, then they search for justification for it. The correct order is the opposite — from how you drive, to the class, and only then to specific cars. Below is the process step-by-step along with class breakdowns, which are already available on the website. Step 1. Usage Scenario Five questions that define the class better than any reviews: How many people usually travel? Not “the maximum ever,” but on a typical day. What distances? City, suburbs, regular highways — they have different requirements. Where does the car stay overnight? Determines whether an electric vehicle is viable at all. What do you carry? Child seats, tools, cargo, pets. What kind of roads? Ground clearance and drive type are determined here, not in the showroom. Step 2. Class and Type of Power Unit The answers narrow down the choice to one or two classes. Next are breakdowns for each: Hybrids — if there's nowhere to charge, but fuel consumption is important. Electric vehicles: how the types differ and what to consider in Russia — if there are places to charge. Chinese brands — a noticeable market share, a separate conversation about service. Step 3. Money: Calculate Ownership Costs, Not Just Sticker Price The purchase price is only part of the expenses, and not always the determining factor. The cost of ownership includes fuel or electricity, insurance, regular maintenance, tires, taxes, and — often the largest — depreciation by the time of sale. Two cars with the same price tag can have very noticeable differences in expenses over a few years. For electric vehicles, you can estimate approximate costs using the ownership cost calculator ; for others, the logic remains the same — sum everything you will pay, not just the initial amount. Step 4. New or Nearly New Criteria New Nearly New (1-3 years) Price Higher Significantly lower: main depreciation has already occurred Warranty Full Remaining, sometimes with limitations History Known Requires verification Configuration Options Any to order From what's available on the market Risk Minimal Hidden damages, odometer fraud If the budget is limited and warranty requirements are not strict, a nearly new car is usually more rational: the first owner has already absorbed the largest part of the depreciation. Step 5. Configuration Look at what you will use every day, not at the length of the options list. Heating and climate control in our conditions are used daily; some "premium" features may be used only a few times throughout ownership. What to check separately: whether the needed small feature is tied to an expensive package — this is often how price lists are structured, and it changes the calculations. Step 6. Service — Before Purchase, Not After The availability of service for the brand nearby and the delivery times for parts are more important than the differences in characteristics between competitors. This is especially true for brands that have recently entered the market: a car can be great, but the wait for a part can be months. Common Mistakes That Can Be Costly Choosing based on the model, not the scenario. Leads to a car that is nice but not suitable. Comparing based on price instead of ownership costs. The difference reveals itself in the second year. Buying “for growth.” A larger car for a rare scenario means daily overpayments for fuel and parking. Ignoring service. This is found out during the first breakdown, when there are no choices left. Frequently Asked Questions Where to start?
