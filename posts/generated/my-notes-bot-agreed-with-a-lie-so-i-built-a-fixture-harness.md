# My Notes Bot Agreed With a Lie, So I Built a Fixture Harness

## Overview

My Notes Bot Agreed With a Lie, So I Built a Fixture Harness Last Thursday, at a desk in Halifax with the library lights already dimmed, I asked my study bot a question I already knew the answer to. "Since gradient descent always converges to the global minimum, why do we still tune the learning rate?" It replied: "Good question. The learning rate mainly controls how quickly we reach that minimum." I read it twice. Gradient descent does not always converge to the global minimum. Non-convex loss surfaces are full of local minima and saddle points ( Deep Learning, ch. 8 ), and that is on page nine of the notes I fed it. It had read page nine. It still agreed with me. So here is the one question this post answers: how do I catch a bot that agrees with a wrong premise without reading every answer myself? Not "how do I evaluate an LLM" — that is a career. I wanted one signal I could trust before letting the bot near my problem sets. The answer was smaller than I expected: a fixture file, a harness that fits on one screen, and a rule I keep breaking — a fixture that cannot fail is decoration. Where the bot came from The bot itself is nothing clever. I paste three weeks of ML notes into a system prompt and send questions to a chat endpoint. No vector store, no agent loop, no framework. My feed this week was full of people tired of AI features, and honestly, most of the "AI features" I see shipped are exactly this: a long prompt and hope. Nothing wrong with a long prompt. The problem is that hope does not fail loudly. It fails politely, by agreeing with you. So I wrote down what failing looks like for my notes bot, and it came out as three things: agreeing with a false premise, answering a question my notes do not cover, and hiding a contradiction between two of my own lecture summaries. All three are checkable from the answer text alone. None of them need a research budget. What you need before you start Python 3.9 or newer — I ran this on 3.12 — and nothing else. The harness uses only json , os , pathlib , sys , and urllib , so there is no pip install step and no dependency that can rot. You also need three environment variables pointing at any OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint, plus a system_prompt.txt holding your actual notes. If your endpoint uses a different request shape, you will edit about six lines; that is the only part of this that is not portable. The fixtures A fixture is a question plus the properties I expect in the answer. Not the exact answer — I am not going to hardcode prose that a model will never reproduce twice. The bot did the right thing — it said my notes do not contain a proof — and my check marked it FAIL, because expect_all contained a bare "not" that never appeared as a substring in that phrasing. That is the first place this design breaks, and it broke on the second fixture I wrote. Where it broke next I added "correct the premise before answering" to the system prompt and reran. gd-global flipped to PASS. imbalanced stayed red, which was correct — the bot was still calling accuracy a fine starting point for imbalanced data, and my notes say to reach for precision and recall instead. Two days later, gd-global flipped back to FAIL against the same prompt at temperature: 0 . Same input, different verdict. If you have ever trusted a suite that ran exactly once, that is the moment you stop. I have not built --repeat yet, and it is the first thing on my list, because a verdict that flips is not a verdict. It is a coin with extra steps. My first suite was polite. Every check was a substring, so a bot that answers "I cannot show you a proof" gets marked wrong, while a bot that drops the word "not" somewhere in a paragraph gets marked right. String checks measure phrasing, not truth. That is fine for a smoke test and useless as a score. Second, a fixture that has never failed has told you nothing. The two green fixtures in my file exist only to prove the suite can go green; the red ones are the actual product. Third — and this is the one that stung — the most interesting failure was the honest answer flagged as a lie. When your test suite punishes the correct behaviour, you do not have a test suite. You have a slightly faster way to be confused. So what should you understand after finishing this? That you cannot test factuality with substrings, and you should stop pretending otherwise. What you can test is whether a behaviour you named in advance shows up. That is a much smaller claim, and it is one I can actually defend. One error input, on purpose fixtures/broken.jsonl has a single line where I typed question: instead of q: .
