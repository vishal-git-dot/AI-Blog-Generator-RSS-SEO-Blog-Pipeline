# High-performance .NET: Span<T>, Memory<T>, System.IO.Pipelines, and channel-based concurrency in real services

## Overview

A practitioner's guide to the four .NET primitives that eliminate allocations in hot paths: Span for stack-sliced parsing, Memory for async-compatible slices, System.IO.Pipelines for backpressure-aware streaming, and System.Threading.Channels for producer-consumer concurrency - with a full working service example that combines all four. The profiler says your service is slow. You look at the flame graph and most of the time is in GC collections, not in your actual business logic. That's the signal that you're allocating more than you need to in the hot path. Four primitives address this at different layers: Span for synchronous slices without heap allocation, Memory for the same across async boundaries, System.IO.Pipelines for network-level parsing without double-buffering, and System.Threading.Channels for producer-consumer pipelines without locks. This isn't an overview. I'll assume you know why string.Substring in a tight loop is a problem. The question is how to actually use these in a service under load. System.IO.Pipelines - partial reads done right The problem Pipelines solves: reading framed messages from a network socket. The classic Stream approach has a subtle correctness problem. You need to buffer the incomplete message, wait for the rest, then process the complete frame. Getting this right means maintaining a circular buffer, tracking read/write positions, and handling the case where a single read gives you 1.5 messages. For most service code - request handlers, repository methods, domain logic - allocation overhead is irrelevant and the constraint that Span can't cross the heap is a maintenance burden. Profile before reaching for this. If your flame graph doesn't show GC pressure in that code path, leave it alone. System.IO.Pipelines is specifically for streaming I/O with partial-read framing. If your network reads always return complete messages (HTTP request bodies, short socket commands with a simple delimiter), a Stream with manual buffering is simpler and correct. Pipelines earn their complexity at high connection counts where the partial-read logic is the hard part. Channel is overkill when producer and consumer run at similar rates with no backpressure risk, or when you just need fire-and-forget background work (Task.Run is fine). Use it when you specifically need bounded capacity, graceful shutdown via Complete(), or async awaiting on an empty queue without polling. The question is always the same: does the profiler show allocation pressure or contention in this specific path? These four primitives are the answer to specific, measurable problems. Used speculatively, they're just complexity.
