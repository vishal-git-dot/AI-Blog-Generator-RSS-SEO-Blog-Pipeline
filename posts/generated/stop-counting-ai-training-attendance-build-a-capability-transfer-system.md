# Stop Counting AI Training Attendance: Build a Capability Transfer System

## Overview

Stop Counting AI Training Attendance: Build a Capability Transfer System An AI workshop is not successful because twenty employees attended, completed a prompt exercise, and rated the instructor 4.7 out of 5. It is successful when people can perform approved work better after the trainer leaves—without leaking data, inventing evidence, automating the wrong decision, or becoming dependent on one enthusiastic colleague. That distinction changes how a Swiss SME should buy, design, and measure AI training. The unit of value is not the training hour. It is a durable, safe capability transferred into a real workflow. The IAPME Suisse guide to AI training and workshops maps institutions, formats, audiences, and workshop content. Its most useful insight is that practical adoption matters more than a certificate for most SMEs. This article turns that idea into an operating system: a role-based capability map, authentic work simulations, evidence rules, coached application, measured transfer, expiry, and retraining. The goal is not to teach everyone “AI.” The goal is to make specific people reliably competent for specific tasks under specific controls. The decision in one sentence Fund AI training only when every module is tied to a real work capability, a safe-use boundary, an observable performance standard, and a post-training transfer test. That rule prevents four common mistakes: buying generic inspiration for teams that need operational practice; teaching tools without teaching data and decision boundaries; measuring confidence instead of performance; assuming a one-day workshop creates a permanent capability. Start with work, not course titles “Prompt engineering,” “AI for leaders,” and “generative AI fundamentals” describe content. They do not describe what the business must be able to do afterward. A useful capability statement has five elements: Actor: who performs the work. Task: what outcome they must produce. Conditions: what tools, information, time, and constraints apply. Standard: what quality and safety threshold counts as competent. Escalation: when the person must stop, verify, or ask for help. For example: A customer-service specialist can use the approved assistant to draft a response from the current knowledge base, verify every policy claim, remove unnecessary personal data, identify uncertainty, and escalate regulated or high-impact cases within eight minutes, with no unsupported promise or unauthorized disclosure. This is trainable and testable. “Understands AI customer service” is not. Build a capability inventory Interview process owners and observe work. Identify repeated tasks where AI is already used, officially or informally. For each task, record frequency, business impact, data classes, current error modes, required judgment, tool access, and the cost of a wrong answer. Then classify the capability: Awareness: recognize possibilities, limits, and prohibited uses. Assisted execution: use an approved tool while retaining human responsibility. Verification: check evidence, calculations, policy, and downstream consequences. Workflow design: configure repeatable prompts, retrieval, or automation safely. Supervision: approve, monitor, investigate, and improve AI-enabled work. Governance: set policy, accept risk, procure systems, and assign accountability. Not everyone needs every level. A board member needs decision literacy, not the same tool practice as a service agent. An automation builder needs deeper failure handling than a casual drafting user. Use Swiss adoption evidence carefully The Swiss SME portal reported that the share of SMEs incorporating AI rose from 22% in 2024 to 34% in 2025 in the cited AXA labour-market study. It also reported that only 34% of companies had clear rules on what data staff may enter into AI tools, falling to 23% among firms with fewer than ten employees ( Swiss SME Portal ). Those figures support a practical conclusion: tool adoption can outrun operating rules. They do not prove that every SME needs the same course, that adoption causes productivity, or that the same percentages apply to your company. Use them as a reason to inspect actual behavior, not as a substitute for a needs assessment. EY's European AI Barometer likewise describes accelerating adoption alongside growing demand for workforce education, and argues that using and calibrating AI tools will become part of lifelong learning ( EY Switzerland ). That is a consulting survey perspective, not a universal causal estimate. It supports continuous capability maintenance; it does not validate a particular provider, course length, or promised return. Your own baseline should answer: Which approved and unapproved tools are people using? For which tasks and data? How often do outputs require correction? What do employees verify, and what do they trust automatically? Where do they copy sensitive information? Which tasks have clear policies and escalation paths? What prevents employees from using the approved path? Anonymous surveys can identify patterns, but observation and artifact review are more reliable than self-reported confidence alone. Protect employee privacy and avoid turning a training assessment into covert surveillance. Design the capability transfer chain Training transfer is the movement from a learning environment into reliable workplace performance. It requires more than content delivery. flowchart LR N[Work needs and risks] --> C[Capability standard] C --> B[Baseline task] B --> L[Learning and practice] L --> S[Safe simulation] S --> P[Coached production use] P --> O[Observed outcome] O --> F[Feedback and remediation] F --> R{Standard met?} R -->|no| L R -->|yes| A[Authorized capability] A --> X[Expiry and revalidation] Every arrow matters. A slide deck without practice produces recognition, not performance. A sandbox without coached use can produce brittle confidence. Production access without verification can convert a learning error into a customer or compliance incident. Authorization without expiry assumes tools and policies remain stable. Baseline before instruction Give participants an authentic task before training. Measure outcome quality, completion time, verification behavior, data handling, escalation, and confidence. Do not grade people publicly. The baseline is used to tailor instruction and calculate change. Use at least two baseline cases: a normal task and an edge case that should trigger caution or escalation. Otherwise the program may optimize speed while missing judgment. Instruction at the point of need Teach only the concepts needed to perform and supervise the target task. A useful sequence is: business outcome and acceptance standard; data and authority boundary; tool mechanics; verification method; failure modes and escalation; practice with feedback; job aid and evidence capture. Explain model uncertainty in operational terms. Employees do not need a lecture on transformer mathematics to understand that fluent output can be wrong, that retrieved text can be stale, or that an instruction embedded in a document may be malicious. Practice across variation One perfect example teaches imitation. Use cases that vary in length, language, ambiguity, missing evidence, customer emotion, and policy sensitivity. Include a case where the correct action is not to use AI. Include another where the output is plausible but contradicted by the source. Coached workplace use After simulation, participants complete a small number of real low-risk tasks with review. The coach checks artifacts and reasoning, not merely whether the tool was opened. Feedback should identify the failed control and the recovery action. Research says environment matters A meta-analysis of 89 empirical studies on training transfer found positive relationships between transfer and factors including motivation and a supportive work environment. It also found that measurement method matters: transfer relationships were consistently inflated when the same source assessed outcomes in the same measurement context ( Journal of Management meta-analysis ). This research is not AI-specific, and it does not supply a universal transfer percentage for your program. It does support two design choices. First, train the environment as well as the learner. Managers must allow time for practice, approved tools must be accessible, policies must be usable, and experts must be available for escalation. An employee cannot transfer a skill when production access is blocked, the safe workflow takes three times longer, or a manager rewards speed while ignoring verification. Second, separate instructor satisfaction from independent performance. The trainer should not be the only person declaring success. Use an agreed rubric, production evidence, process-owner review, and downstream outcomes. If the participant, trainer, and manager all rely on the same self-rating, confidence can masquerade as capability. Define an AI work standard Each trained workflow needs a short standard that an employee can apply under pressure. Use the following structure. Purpose State the permitted outcome. Example: draft an internal summary for a human decision, not make the decision. Inputs List approved sources and prohibited data. Explain how to minimize personal or confidential information before submission. Tool and configuration Name the approved product, account type, workspace, retrieval source, and important settings. “Use ChatGPT” is incomplete if personal accounts are prohibited. Required checks Specify how to verify facts, calculations, citations, names, dates, and policy. A check should be observable. “Use judgment” is not enough. Authority State what the user may publish, send, change, or approve. Drafting authority is not transaction authority. Escalation List triggers: missing evidence, regulated advice, sensitive data, customer dispute, unexpected tool behavior, or high-impact action. Evidence and retention Define what proof is stored, where, for how long, and who may access it. Do not preserve full sensitive prompts merely to prove that training occurred. Segment the curriculum by role Executives Executives should learn how to frame outcomes, identify unacceptable risk, interpret evidence, challenge vendor claims, assign ownership, and stop weak pilots. Their assessment should be a decision exercise, not a prompt contest. Give them a proposal with attractive ROI, incomplete baseline data, unclear data flows, and no exit plan. Ask them to approve, reject, or conditionally admit it. Score the quality of their questions and decision. Managers and process owners Managers need workflow decomposition, acceptance criteria, capacity planning, human-review design, incident handling, and measurement. They should be able to distinguish a model error from a broken process or unavailable source. Their assessment can require a supervised workflow map, risk register, and evaluation plan for one team task. Frontline users Frontline training should focus on approved tasks, data minimization, instruction quality, source verification, output editing, escalation, and recovery. Use their actual document types and realistic time pressure. Their assessment must include an unsafe or unsupported case that requires refusal or escalation. Builders and champions Champions need deeper knowledge of prompt and context design, retrieval, tool permissions, evaluation, versioning, monitoring, and support. They also need teaching and triage skills. A champion is not an informal administrator with unlimited access. Their assessment should include diagnosing a failing workflow, not just building a successful demonstration. Security, privacy, legal, and HR Control functions need a shared vocabulary and a review method that is fast enough to be used. They should practice data-flow review, purpose limitation, access decisions, employee impact, vendor evidence, incident response, and proportional controls. Do not train these groups in isolation. Cross-functional simulation exposes conflicting assumptions before they become production delays. Measure capability at four levels Level 1: knowledge Can the participant explain limits, approved uses, data rules, and escalation? Use scenario questions rather than definitions. Level 2: simulated performance Can the participant complete representative tasks in a controlled environment? Measure artifact quality and process compliance. Level 3: workplace transfer Can the participant repeat performance on real approved work after days or weeks? Measure independent artifacts and supervisor-observed behavior. Level 4: business and risk outcome Does the workflow improve accepted output, cycle time, rework, customer outcome, safety, or capacity without shifting hidden work downstream? Do not jump from Level 1 quiz scores to Level 4 ROI claims. Establish the causal chain and retain alternative explanations. A seasonal demand change, new template, staffing change, or parallel software release can affect outcomes. Calculate training value honestly Use a before-and-after comparison at the accepted-work boundary: net_training_value = accepted_output_value + avoided_rework + avoided_incident_loss + released_capacity_value - course_and_trainer_cost - participant_time - coaching_and_review_time - tool_and_integration_cost - disruption_and_maintenance Avoid counting every “hour saved” at full salary value. Released time creates value only if it is used productively or reduces a real constraint. Do not double-count faster drafting and higher throughput if they describe the same benefit. Include the manager, champion, security, and review time required to sustain the capability. Use a comparison group or staggered rollout when feasible. At minimum, preserve the baseline, workload volume, quality floor, and contextual changes. Report ranges rather than unjustified precision. The IAPME source includes indicative productivity and payback claims. Treat those as hypotheses to test in your workflow, not guaranteed outcomes. The source itself emphasizes role-specific exercises and follow-up; those design elements are more transferable than any generic ROI number. Apply an evidence gate to training claims Training proposals often promise hours saved, rapid payback, adoption multipliers, or dramatic risk reduction. Before buying, ask what population was studied, what baseline was used, how “productivity” was defined, how long outcomes were observed, who measured them, and whether review or rework was included. A testimonial or satisfaction average does not establish a likely operational result. The US Federal Trade Commission's small-business advertising guidance says objective claims need a reasonable basis and objective supporting evidence; the required evidence depends on the claim ( FTC advertising guidance ). The legal application of that guidance depends on jurisdiction and facts, so obtain appropriate advice. The broader procurement discipline travels well: match the strength of a promise to the strength and relevance of its evidence. Create a claim register before contract signature. For every material promise, record the exact wording, evidence supplied, measurement population, assumptions, exclusions, owner, and verification date. Convert unsupported claims into pilot hypotheses. For example, replace “participants save two hours a day” with “during a four-week controlled pilot, eligible participants will reduce median accepted completion time on workflow X by at least 15%, without increasing critical errors or reviewer minutes.” This protects both buyer and provider. The buyer avoids budgeting fictional capacity. A responsible provider gets a fair, predeclared test instead of being judged on anecdotes or shifting expectations. If a claim cannot be expressed as an observable outcome, remove it from the business case. Build a reinforcement loop Job aids Provide a one-page standard, approved prompt or form, verification checklist, escalation path, and example of a good evidence packet. Job aids reduce memory burden and improve consistency. Office hours Run short weekly sessions during the first month. Collect recurring problems and update standards. Do not let office hours become an untracked exception channel. Peer review Pair users for early tasks, rotate reviewers, and sample later work. Peer review helps distribute knowledge but needs a rubric to avoid propagating confident mistakes. Champion network Assign time, scope, and escalation support to champions. Measure resolved issues and improvements, not the number of tips posted in chat. Change triggers Require revalidation after material changes to the model, tool, data source, policy, workflow, permissions, or output use. A certificate from six months ago does not prove competence on a newly autonomous workflow. Skill retention Retest critical judgment after a delay. Include a task that must be completed without AI so the organization can detect overdependence and preserve fallback competence. Contradictions and trade-offs Standardization versus authentic work Standard tests improve comparison, but overly generic tasks predict little about the workplace. Keep common gates while varying role-specific cases. Tool-specific practice versus durable literacy Employees need hands-on familiarity with the approved interface. They also need transferable skills: task framing, data minimization, evidence checks, uncertainty, and escalation. Teach both layers explicitly. Psychological safety versus accountability People must be able to reveal mistakes and shadow use without immediate punishment, or risks stay hidden. The company must still enforce deliberate policy violations and high-impact negligence. Publish the distinction and remediation process. Speed versus verification Training often demonstrates rapid drafting, but verification can consume the apparent saving. Measure total accepted-work time. Improve retrieval and templates rather than pressuring users to skip checks. Broad access versus controlled exposure Practice drives learning, yet unrestricted production access is unsafe. Use realistic sandboxes, synthetic or minimized data, and staged privileges. Champion-led adoption versus key-person dependency Champions accelerate support, but an SME can become dependent on one person. Version standards, rotate roles, document decisions, and ensure coverage. Failure modes and controls Failure mode: attendance success The dashboard reports learners and completion, but no workplace outcome. Control: require simulation, coached tasks, delayed transfer checks, and accepted-work measures. Failure mode: confidence inflation Participants feel more capable but cannot detect plausible errors. Control: compare confidence with observed accuracy and include deceptive cases. Failure mode: prompt cargo cult Employees memorize a “perfect prompt” without understanding task boundaries. Control: vary cases and assess diagnosis, evidence, and escalation. Failure mode: policy as a slide Data rules appear once in the course and are unavailable during work. Control: embed policy into tools, job aids, examples, and gates. Failure mode: unsafe practice data Participants paste real confidential material into personal accounts during exercises. Control: provision approved sandboxes and synthetic or minimized cases before training. Failure mode: no manager transfer The employee learns a method, but workload, incentives, and approvals block use. Control: include managers, allocate practice time, and align performance expectations. Failure mode: trainer self-certification The provider designs, delivers, and exclusively judges success. Control: pre-agree independent rubrics and process-owner validation. Failure mode: permanent badge Authorization survives material changes to tools or responsibilities. Control: use expiry dates and event-triggered revalidation. Failure mode: automation escalation A drafting course evolves into autonomous sending without new assessment. Control: classify authority levels and require a separate gate for every added action. Failure mode: hidden inequality Training reaches already confident staff while others lose access to development. Control: examine participation, support, outcomes, and remediation by relevant groups, with privacy safeguards. A practical 60-day rollout Days 1–10: diagnose Select one workflow, observe baseline performance, map risk, define the standard, and identify participant and manager needs. Days 11–20: design Build role-specific instruction, normal and edge cases, a safe sandbox, rubric, job aid, escalation route, and capability ledger. Days 21–25: teach and simulate Deliver short foundations, demonstrate the complete workflow, practice with feedback, and assess independently. Days 26–40: coached work Authorize low-risk production use with review. Hold office hours, capture recurring failures, and improve controls. Days 41–50: measure transfer Sample real artifacts, compare baseline and current accepted work, interview process owners, and identify hidden review or rework. Days 51–60: decide Authorize, restrict, remediate, or stop the workflow. Publish the standard, ledger status, outcome evidence, and next revalidation trigger. Expand only after the first capability holds. Validation checklist Before calling an AI training program successful, verify: [ ] A real workflow and owner are named. [ ] Capability statements define actor, task, conditions, standard, and escalation. [ ] Baseline performance was measured before instruction. [ ] Normal, edge, adversarial, and no-AI cases are included. [ ] Approved tools and account boundaries are explicit. [ ] Practice data are synthetic, minimized, or properly authorized. [ ] Data, privacy, security, and authority rules are embedded in practice. [ ] Required verification steps are observable. [ ] Executives, managers, users, builders, and control functions receive role-appropriate training. [ ] Hard safety gates cannot be offset by speed or confidence. [ ] Simulation performance is assessed with an agreed rubric. [ ] Real low-risk tasks are completed with coaching. [ ] Workplace transfer is checked after a delay. [ ] Business value is measured at the accepted-output boundary. [ ] Review, rework, coaching, and maintenance costs are included. [ ] Instructor satisfaction is separated from independent performance. [ ] Job aids and escalation support are available during work. [ ] Managers provide time and incentives for safe practice. [ ] Capability authorization has limits, evidence, and expiry. [ ] Material tool or workflow changes trigger revalidation. [ ] Employees retain manual fallback and critical judgment. [ ] Participation and remediation are fair and privacy-respecting. The final decision rule Buy the shortest learning intervention that can produce and sustain the required workplace capability. Reject generic training when the provider cannot connect content to authentic tasks, safe-use rules, independent assessment, transfer evidence, and follow-up. A good workshop can start the process. It cannot be the whole process. Capability exists only when people can perform approved work to a defined standard, explain the limits, recover from failure, and repeat that performance after the workshop glow has faded.
