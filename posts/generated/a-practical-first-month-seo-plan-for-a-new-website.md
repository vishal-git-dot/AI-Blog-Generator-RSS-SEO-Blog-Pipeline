# A Practical First-Month SEO Plan for a New Website

## Overview

A new website does not need a hundred SEO tasks on day one. It needs a reliable way for people to reach important pages, content that answers actual questions, and a measurement setup that connects visits to useful actions. The first 30 days are best treated as a preparation and learning period. There is no guaranteed date by which a search engine will index every page or rank the site for a particular query. The schedule below is a practical sequence, not a promise of traffic. If you discover a serious access or tracking problem, fix it before moving on. Days 1–3: define the job and map the pages Write down what the business actually offers, who it serves, where it operates, and what a visitor should do next. A service company may want a quote request; a store may want a completed order. Make those outcomes specific enough that someone can test the journey. Create a simple page inventory with a URL or planned page, the visitor's task, publication status, owner, and next check. A small service site might begin with: A home page that explains the offer and leads to relevant services. A service page with scope, genuine conditions, and a contact path. A contact page with current details. An educational article that answers a question customers ask before they buy. Mark planned addresses separately from live ones. If a site is already public, do not rename URLs merely for aesthetics without considering existing links and a redirect plan. Check the preferred domain and protocol variants as part of the launch review. Days 4–7: verify access and crawlability Open important pages on desktop and mobile. Check whether text is readable, navigation works, buttons go where they promise, and the site loads securely. Test the contact form and trace the inquiry to the person responsible for responding. A search visit is not useful if a prospective customer cannot complete the next step. Inspect the server responses for important addresses and make sure missing pages are treated as missing rather than presented as successful pages with an error message. Check redirects and links after any corrections. Review robots.txt and page-level indexing settings with someone who understands the site. Robots.txt controls crawler access; it is not a substitute for authentication or protection of confidential information. If a page needs a noindex instruction, the crawler must be able to access the instruction to read it. Test changes on a small set of pages before applying them broadly. An XML sitemap can list the preferred, live pages that you want search engines to discover. Exclude test URLs, error pages, and unnecessary parameter variants. Submit or reference the sitemap through the relevant webmaster tools and monitor their reports. A sitemap helps communicate URLs; it does not guarantee indexing. Days 8–14: connect questions to the right pages Start with your actual products and services, customer questions, and the language people use to describe the problem. Use search research tools and any existing first-party data to refine the list. Record the source, region, and date for numerical estimates; do not invent volume or traffic forecasts when data is missing. Group similar queries by the task they express. Someone comparing air-conditioning installation prices may need a service page with clear conditions. Someone learning how to choose a unit may need an educational guide. Both topics relate to the same business, but the expected answer can differ. Create a “task → page” map. One useful page can address several closely related phrases. A separate URL for every synonym creates unnecessary maintenance. Conversely, placing every unrelated service and question on one page can make the answer hard to find. For each priority page, record what information is missing, which existing page could satisfy the task, and what the reader should do next. Treat intent as a working hypothesis to revisit when useful search data arrives. Days 15–21: make the important pages useful A service page should explain the work, the area served, meaningful limitations, and the contact process. A product page should describe current options and purchase conditions. Use actual business information rather than inventing prices, delivery promises, reviews, or case studies. Give each key page a descriptive title and a clear main heading. Write a meta description that accurately summarizes the page and distinguishes it from others. Avoid repeating a keyword unnaturally or promising a service the company does not offer. A search engine may display a different snippet, so a description is not a guarantee of what will appear in results. Use images when they add information and provide meaningful alternative text where appropriate. Select a page format based on what the visitor needs rather than a fixed word count. A useful guide, a comparison page, and a service page each have different jobs. Avoid publishing many near-identical placeholders simply to increase the page count. A smaller collection of accurate, maintainable pages is a more useful starting point for a new site. Days 22–30: connect the journey and establish measurement Make sure important pages are reachable through clear navigation and ordinary internal links. An article about choosing equipment can link to a related installation service; the service page can link to relevant questions and contact information. Link text should tell readers where they are going. Verify the site in the webmaster tools available for the search engines that matter to the audience, such as Google Search Console or Yandex Webmaster. Inspect a sample of important URLs, investigate reported problems in context, and recheck after making changes. Reports can take time to update. Configure analytics with appropriate attention to applicable data-handling requirements. Agree on the events that matter: a submitted form, a phone-link click, a checkout start, or a confirmed order. A click on a phone link is not automatically a completed call, so document what each event actually measures and avoid counting the same inquiry twice. For illustration, suppose a new site receives 100 organic visits, five form submissions, and two inquiries that meet the team's qualification criteria. These numbers are examples, not benchmarks. In a small sample, one additional inquiry changes the percentages substantially. Compare similar periods and traffic sources, and note incomplete tracking or seasonality. Day 30: a review checklist By the end of the first month, aim to answer these questions: Are the priority pages listed, owned, and assigned a visitor task? Do the site, navigation, important links, and forms work on desktop and mobile? Have crawl settings, indexing instructions, sitemap entries, and URL variants been reviewed? Is each major query group mapped to a useful page or an explicitly planned page? Are titles, descriptions, claims, contact details, and service terms accurate? Are webmaster tools and meaningful events configured, with their limitations recorded? Is there a prioritized issue list with an owner and a date for the next check? Continue after day 30: improve pages, investigate technical problems, refine internal links, and review the quality of inquiries. The first month produces a baseline and a work list; it does not finish search optimization.
