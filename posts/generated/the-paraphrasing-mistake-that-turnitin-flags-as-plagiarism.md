# The Paraphrasing Mistake That Turnitin Flags as Plagiarism

## Overview

You did everything "right." You read the source, closed the tab, wrote the idea in your own words, cited the author. Then your Turnitin report comes back at 34% similarity and your guide wants a meeting. This is one of the most common — and most misunderstood — false alarms in academic writing. Except it's not always a false alarm. Let's break down what's actually happening. The Mistake: Patchwriting There's a name for this in academic integrity literature: patchwriting. It's the gap between paraphrasing and copying, and most researchers fall into it without realizing. Patchwriting looks like this — take the original sentence, swap a few words for synonyms, maybe restructure the clause order slightly, keep the same sentence architecture and logical flow. The result reads like your own writing. Turnitin's similarity algorithm doesn't care. It's not checking whether words are identical — it's checking sentence structure, phrase sequencing, and pattern similarity. Patchwriting trips every one of those checks. Original: Climate change has significantly altered precipitation patterns across South Asia, leading to more frequent extreme weather events. * Patchwritten (still flagged): * Climate change has considerably changed rainfall patterns throughout South Asia, resulting in more common extreme weather occurrences. Different words. Same skeleton. Turnitin flags this correctly — this is not paraphrasing, it's synonym substitution. Why This Happens (It's Not Carelessness) Most researchers don't patchwrite because they're being lazy. It happens because of how paraphrasing is actually done under time pressure: You read the source sentence The source sentence is still sitting in your working memory, structurally intact You write "your version" while the original's sentence shape is still the scaffold you're building on This is a cognitive problem, not an ethics problem, in most cases. But the tool — and your committee — can't distinguish intent from outcome. The Actual Fix: Read, Close, Recall, Write The technique that reliably breaks patchwriting is deceptively simple: Read the source passage fully — don't paraphrase sentence-by-sentence as you go Close the source. Physically. Don't have it open in a split window "just to check" Recall the idea from memory and write it down without looking back Compare afterward — check the source only after you've written your version, to catch accidental structural overlap This forces your brain to reconstruct the idea, not the sentence. Because you literally cannot see the source while writing, you can't inherit its syntax. The Threshold Question: What Actually Counts as a Violation This is where a lot of scholars get confused, and where India-specific context matters. Under UGC's 2018 regulations, plagiarism severity is tiered: Below 10% — generally acceptable, no penalty (this includes quotes, references, bibliography — most tools exclude these automatically) 10–40% — minor penalty, resubmission usually required within a set timeframe 40–60% — more serious penalty, can affect degree progression Above 60% — can result in suspension or expulsion, depending on the institution A 34% similarity score isn't automatically catastrophic — but it's high enough that your committee will want to see where the overlap is concentrated. If it's clustered in a few paraphrased paragraphs from one or two sources, that's a patchwriting pattern, and that's fixable. If it's scattered thinly across dozens of sources, that's usually just common phrasing and citations, and far less concerning. A Quick Pre-Submission Check Before you submit anything through your institution's Turnitin account: Run it through a checker yourself first, if you have access to one, so you're not seeing your score for the first time alongside your guide Look at where the flagged overlap sits — literature review sections are almost always the highest-risk zone, since that's where most paraphrasing happens For any paragraph flagged at high similarity, apply the read-close-recall-write method and rewrite from scratch — don't edit the flagged sentence, replace it entirely Double-check that block quotes are properly formatted (quotation marks + citation) — sometimes what gets flagged is a legitimate quote that's just missing formatting, not a paraphrasing issue at all The Real Takeaway A high similarity score isn't a verdict — it's a diagnostic. The mistake worth understanding isn't "I copied something," it's "I paraphrased too close to the source's original structure without realizing it." Once you can see the difference, it's a completely fixable habit, not a character flaw.
