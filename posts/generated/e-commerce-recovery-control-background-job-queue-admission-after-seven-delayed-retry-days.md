# E-Commerce Recovery Control: Background Job Queue Admission After Seven Delayed Retry Days

## Overview

A rate-limited worker pool changes the scheduling question. If a large e-commerce background job queue backlog becomes eligible at once, admitting every delayed retry can recreate the overload that caused it. Short answer: keep retry eligibility in a durable ledger, let cron wake a bounded dispatcher, and enqueue only the work the pool can absorb. The queue should own ready work, while the ledger owns waits that exceed the queue's delayed-message limit. The evaluation constraint is operational recovery: after dispatch resumes, the oldest valid job must get younger without saturating the workers or repeating an expensive side effect. A simple delayed timer can pass a timing test and still fail that recovery test. I don't count an enqueue acknowledgment as success; for AI-generated catalog work, the output also has to pass the same schema and content evals as a first attempt. That distinction matters. Start with recovery invariants, not a longer timer Imagine a catalog enrichment pool paused while supplier data is unavailable. Product records continue to accumulate retry dates, and some dates are weeks away. When those records become due, the system has two jobs: preserve the intended eligibility time and control how quickly due work returns to service. A queue delay handles the first only while the delay remains inside its supported window. It does not, by itself, solve the second. The tempting approach is to split a long wait into a chain of shorter delayed messages. That creates ambiguous state. A product can change revision during the chain; an operator can pause the campaign; a message can be delivered more than once; and the worker pool can still receive the entire cohort at the final boundary. Each hop also makes it harder to answer a basic recovery question: which records are due but have not yet been offered? Use explicit invariants instead: A stable job ID identifies the logical side effect across every offer. next_attempt_at is durable application state, not an in-memory timer. The dispatcher admits no more than its current budget on one tick. A product revision is either pinned or rejected as superseded by an explicit policy. Completion is checked before the costly dependency is called. These rules do not promise exactly-once execution across a database and a queue. They make repeated offers harmless, which is the useful contract when claiming a database record and publishing a message cannot share one transaction. How should a background job queue run scheduled retry after a delayed message limit? Use two time horizons. For short waits that fit the queue's documented delayed-delivery window, native delay keeps the system small. For waits beyond that boundary, store a retry row with a stable ID, eligibility timestamp, attempt count, revision, state, and lease. A periodic trigger invokes a dispatcher; it does not become the clock of record. The dispatcher atomically claims a limited group of eligible rows. Each claim has an expiry so an interrupted invocation does not strand work forever. It then publishes the stable ID and immutable execution inputs to the ready queue. Once an offer is recorded, the normal worker path takes over. Cron is allowed to run twice, arrive late, or overlap with another invocation because correctness comes from atomic claims and idempotent work. The schedule controls how often capacity is reconsidered; it must not determine whether a record exists. This is why a cron-only design, where the job list lives in configuration, becomes awkward as soon as operators need per-record cancellation, inspection, or rescheduling. Keep classification ahead of scheduling too. A temporary rate-limit response can move next_attempt_at . Invalid product input should become terminal or enter review. Giving malformed input a longer clock turns a data-quality problem into future load, and no retry mechanism fixes that. I'm not sure a fixed cron interval is right for every store; burst shape, recovery deadline, downstream limits, and oldest-due age are the evidence needed to choose it. My notebook-sized fixture would seed five rows: three due, one due tomorrow, and one complete. With an admission budget of two, the first tick must offer two due rows. A repeated tick must not repeat either completed side effect. Next, expire a claim before mark_offered , advance the clock, and verify that the same stable ID becomes eligible for another offer. Finally, change one product revision while its retry is waiting and assert the chosen supersession policy. That last case catches an expensive mistake. Queue mechanics can be perfectly correct while an old generation request overwrites newer catalog input. Record token use by stable job ID and attempt, and run output evals after every execution; otherwise duplicate offers and low-quality retries disappear inside an apparently healthy throughput chart. Drain the backlog as a feedback loop An admission budget should respond to observed headroom, not to the number of due rows alone. Start a recovery drill with a low limit, observe worker saturation and completion, then raise or hold the next release. Fresh orders may need a reserved share of capacity so a historical catalog backlog cannot monopolize the pool. Fairness can also require ordering by eligibility time rather than repeatedly selecting newer, easier jobs. The primary progress signal is the age of the oldest eligible record. Pair it with accepted outputs per attempt, worker utilization, claims per tick, lease expirations, duplicate suppression, terminal input failures, and token use. Queue depth is secondary: a deep queue can be draining normally, while a shallow queue can hide one job that has been skipped for days. Go slowly. Before copying this design, run a controlled drill: pause dispatch, accumulate due records, resume with a small budget, invoke the dispatcher twice, interrupt once between enqueue and mark_offered , and supersede one product revision. The pass condition is not merely that messages move. Oldest-due age must fall, the pool must remain under its chosen saturation boundary, completed effects must not repeat, and regenerated content must still clear the eval suite. The catch is the durable-ledger design adds a state model, atomic claiming, lease cleanup, dashboards, and an idempotency contract shared by producers and workers. It is not suitable when the team cannot operate those semantics. Stick with native delayed delivery when every wait fits the supported window and there is no need for independent inspection or paced recovery. A durable workflow system is another reasonable category when long waits and resumable steps are central, but it brings its own execution model and operational surface. Choose from the recovery evidence. A cron-triggered ledger earns its complexity only when a rehearsal shows that bounded admission, explicit state, and stable IDs solve a problem the ready queue cannot express alone.
