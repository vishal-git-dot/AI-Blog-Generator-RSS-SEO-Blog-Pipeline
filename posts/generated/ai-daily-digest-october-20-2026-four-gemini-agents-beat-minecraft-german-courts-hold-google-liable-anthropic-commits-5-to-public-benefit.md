Four Gemini 4 Argon Agents Beat Minecraft With No Human-Written Game Logic Google DeepMind reported a result that reads simple and lands heavy: four Gemini 4 Argon agents, dropped into a fresh Minecraft survival world, defeated the Ender Dragon in 27 hours and 14 minutes. Nobody assigned roles. Nobody wrote game logic. The agents played through Mineflayer, an open source JavaScript library, and coordinated through in-game chat plus a shared memory store. Along the way they wrote 2,783 programs, died 605 times, and exchanged 229 chat messages. The paper, arXiv 2610.22319, calls it the first end-to-end autonomous completion of Minecraft using only the standard library, with no pre-scouted seeds and no game-specific tools written by humans. — Google DeepMind · The Decoder The behavior under the hood matters more than the boss fight. With no captain appointed, the four agents split the work in chat within the first ten minutes: one on tools, one on food, one on diamonds, one on the Nether portal. They logged coordinates and lessons to shared notes, retrieved items dropped by dead teammates, and averaged one new program roughly every 35 seconds for over a day. The median program ran 33.5 seconds. That is long-horizon planning, error recovery, and coordination sustained across thousands of dependent steps, which is exactly the shape of real software engineering and enterprise work. — Google DeepMind The caveats are in the paper, and they are worth reading before quoting the headline. This was a single run on Easy difficulty. The agents never saw pixels or touched a keyboard: Mineflayer exposes privileged internal game state, and the setup used a pathfinding plugin. An earlier project, Altoclef, beat Minecraft autonomously back in 2021, though with hand-authored task logic rather than code written at runtime by general-purpose agents. The honest framing is not "AI beat Minecraft first." It is that a generalist coding team organizes itself, recovers from hundreds of failures, and finishes a multi-day task with no script, and that capability transfers. — Google DeepMind 🔗 arXiv 2610.22319 · The Decoder 2. Two German Courts Rule Google's AI Overviews Are Its Own Content Two German regional courts, Munich I and Aachen, have now held Google directly liable for false statements in its AI Overviews. The Munich case started in May, when the AI tied two publishers to scams and subscription traps they had nothing to do with. The court classified AI Overviews as Google's own statements rather than a list of links, making the company a direct infringer instead of a neutral conduit. Google announced an appeal, then settled quietly, so no higher court has reviewed the reasoning. — Munich Regional Court · Aachen Regional Court · The Decoder The Aachen ruling shows the template traveling. An AI Mode summary attributed a sexual assault case to a neuroradiology physician when the criminal proceedings actually involved a different person, and invented a complete legal aftermath. The doctor asked Google to remove the content in late August; Google declined in mid-September and the false text stayed live. On September 24 the Aachen Regional Court issued an emergency injunction relying explicitly on the Munich reasoning, with fines up to 250,000 euros per violation if Google keeps spreading the statements. — Aachen Regional Court · The Decoder The legal move that does the damage is narrow and portable. Courts distinguished AI summaries from the search-engine case law that previously shielded Google: a search engine points to outside websites, while an AI Overview generates independent, new, substantive statements that only Google can check against its sources. The court also noted users almost never click the citations, and rejected the argument that AI output is protected expression, since it is the result of an algorithm rather than an acquired conviction. If this line holds, every answer engine operating in Europe inherits the same exposure. — The Decoder 🔗 The Decoder 5. Google Research Open-Sources Harness2 for Tuning Agent Harnesses Without Labels Google Research published Harness2 v0.1.0, an open source toolkit for improving the harness around a frozen model, the prompts, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts, and plugins, without verifiers or labeled data. For each task it runs a contrastive Propose, Probe, Compose loop: it tests a general-purpose harness edit against a domain-specific one, keeps only changes backed by observed behavior, and stores lessons in an EXPERIENCE.md file that future tasks can draw on. — Google Research · GitHub The numbers in the paper are modest in size and interesting in shape. A single refinement step improved all four model-and-harness combinations across all three benchmarks, with gains from +11.5 points down to +1.2. One setup, GLM-5 under OpenCode, went from 64.2 to 75.7. The pattern worth noting is that the method optimizes the scaffolding while the model weights stay frozen, which is where most teams actually live: you cannot retrain the model, but you can restructure how it is prompted, tooled, and checked. — Google Research For teams building agents in production, the useful idea is not the specific tool but the discipline it encodes. Harness changes are usually shipped on vibes and reverted after incidents. Harness2 makes each change earn its place through measured behavior on real tasks, then accumulates that evidence in a file the agent itself reads. That loop, propose, probe, keep only what works, remember why, is a reasonable default for anyone maintaining an agent stack in 2026. — Google Research 🔗 google-research/harness2 KD Agentic · AI Daily Digest
