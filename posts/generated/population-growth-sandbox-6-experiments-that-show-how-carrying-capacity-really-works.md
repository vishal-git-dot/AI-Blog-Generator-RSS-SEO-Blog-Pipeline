# Population Growth Sandbox: 6 Experiments That Show How Carrying Capacity Really Works

## Overview

In 1911, a small herd of reindeer was released on St. Paul Island, Alaska, a place with plenty of lichen and no predators. By most accounts the herd grew into the low thousands within a few decades, ate the island's slow-growing pasture down to almost nothing, and then collapsed to a tiny remnant. (The exact figures vary by source, so treat these as a rough shape rather than a dataset.) That story is the whole idea behind carrying capacity in one picture: growth that feels unstoppable, a ceiling nobody can see until they hit it, and a crash when a population overshoots. Textbooks compress this into one equation. Equations don't build intuition, though. Turning the knobs does. So this post is a lab notebook. We'll take one small population, run six experiments on it, change one variable at a time, and watch what breaks. The equipment: four numbers and two equations Everything below uses four parameters: Symbol Meaning N₀ Starting population size r Intrinsic growth rate, per time step K Carrying capacity, the maximum the environment can sustain t Time And two models. Exponential growth (unlimited resources): dN/dt = rN → N(t) = N₀ · e^(rt) Logistic growth (limited resources), introduced by Pierre François Verhulst in 1838: dN/dt = rN · (K − N) / K N(t) = K / (1 + ((K − N₀) / N₀) · e^(−rt)) The only difference is the braking term (K − N) / K. It's close to 1 when the population is tiny (so growth looks exponential), it shrinks toward 0 as N approaches K (so growth stops), and it turns negative if N is above K (so the population shrinks). The second line is the "integrated" or closed-form solution. It gives the population at any time directly, with no step-by-step simulation. That detail matters a lot in Experiment 6. Three derived quantities will come up repeatedly: Inflection point: growth is fastest when N = K/2 . Maximum growth rate: (dN/dt)ₘₐₓ = rK/4 . Time to reach the inflection point: t = (1/r) · ln((K − N₀) / N₀) . Our test population for every experiment unless stated otherwise: N₀ = 10, K = 1,000, r = 0.5 per time step. This is the central difficulty of estimating carrying capacity from early data (more on that later). Peak growth is at t ≈ 9.19, when N = 500 exactly. That's K/2, and the growth rate there is 125, which equals rK/4 = 0.5 × 1000 / 4. The population is 99.6% of K by t = 20, but it never quite reaches 1,000. It approaches K asymptotically. The growth rate drops toward zero because the braking term is nearly zero. Exponential growth is absurd by t = 20. It predicts over 220,000 individuals in an environment that can support 1,000. This is the classic S-shaped (sigmoid) curve, as opposed to the J-shaped exponential one. Try it: enter N₀ = 10, r = 0.5, K = 1000 in the calculator and check the summary metrics. You should see an inflection point of 500, a max growth rate of 125, and an inflection time of 9.1902. Experiment 1: turn up the growth rate (r) Hypothesis: A higher r means a higher carrying capacity. Let's test it. Keep N₀ = 10 and K = 1000, and vary r: r Time to inflection Max growth rate (rK/4) Time to reach 90% of K 0.25 18.38 62.5 27.17 0.50 9.19 125.0 13.58 1.00 4.60 250.0 6.79 2.00 2.30 500.0 3.40 Result: Hypothesis rejected. K never moved. All four populations end up at 1,000. What r controls is how fast you get there. Notice the pattern: doubling r halves every time value (18.38 → 9.19 → 4.60 → 2.30) and doubles the peak growth rate. This follows from the equations, since r only appears as e^(−rt) and as a multiplier. Time scales as 1/r. The lesson is about biotic potential . A fast-breeding species (bacteria, insects, rodents) reaches its ceiling quickly, but the ceiling is set by resources, not by how fast the species can reproduce. A slow-breeding species (elephants, whales) gets to its K more slowly but has the same fundamental limit. Experiment 2: change the starting population (N₀) Hypothesis: Starting with more individuals raises where the population ends up. Same setup (K = 1000, r = 0.5), different starting sizes: N₀ Time to inflection Time to 90% of K 1 13.81 18.21 10 9.19 13.58 100 4.39 8.79 400 0.81 5.21 500 0 (already at K/2) 4.39 800 n/a (already past K/2) 1.62 Result: Rejected again. N₀ shifts the timeline , not the destination. Every row ends at 1,000. Two details are worth catching: Once N₀ ≥ K/2, there's no inflection point in the future. The population is already at or beyond its fastest-growth moment, so it starts in the decelerating phase. A well-built calculator should handle this case explicitly, and this tool's page says it notes when the inflection point doesn't apply. A tiny founder population (N₀ = 1) is slow, but not weak. It takes about 18 time steps to reach 90% of K, versus 13.6 for N₀ = 10. The destination is the same. This also explains post-disaster recovery. After a fire or storm knocks a population down, it climbs back along the same S-curve, and recovery is slowest when numbers are lowest. Experiment 3: raise the ceiling (K) Hypothesis: Doubling K doubles everything. Keep N₀ = 10 and r = 0.5, and vary K: K Time to inflection Max growth rate (rK/4) 500 7.78 62.5 1,000 9.19 125.0 2,000 10.59 250.0 Result: Half right. Peak growth rate doubles each time, because it's proportional to K. But the time to inflection only goes up by about 1.39 each time you double K. That number isn't random. It's ln(2) / r = 0.693 / 0.5 ≈ 1.386. A population living in an environment twice as large doesn't take twice as long to hit its peak growth. It takes a fixed extra amount, because early growth is exponential and doubling a target on an exponential curve costs a constant amount of time. The ecological reading: a richer environment gives you a bigger ceiling and a higher peak growth rate, but it doesn't slow the early rise much. Experiment 4: start above the ceiling (overshoot) Hypothesis: If N₀ is greater than K, the population will decline smoothly to K. Set N₀ = 1000, K = 500, r = 0.2: t N dN/dt 0 1,000.00 −200.00 5 612.70 −27.62 10 536.29 −7.78 15 512.76 −2.62 20 504.62 −0.93 30 500.62 −0.12 40 500.08 −0.02 Result: Confirmed. The braking term (K − N) / K is negative when N > K, so dN/dt is negative. The population shrinks fast at first (−200 per step) and slows as it approaches K from above. This is the "dieback" phase. Now the limitation , which is important. This model shows a smooth return to K. It never undershoots, never oscillates, never crashes below the ceiling. Real populations often do, as the St. Paul Island reindeer story shows. Why? Because real populations have time lags . Resources get consumed now, but starvation shows up later. By the time the population "notices" it's over capacity, it's already far over, and the correction is brutal. The basic logistic equation assumes the brake responds instantly to current density. Delayed-response models are needed for the boom-and-bust cycles seen in the wild, and that's a good reminder of what a simple model leaves out. Experiment 5: how fast is the fastest? Before the last experiment, let's check three homework-style problems, since these are exactly the questions a calculator helps you verify. Problem A: instantaneous growth rate. Field mice have r = 0.15 per year, K = 800, and the current population is N = 200. What is dN/dt? dN/dt = 0.15 × 200 × (800 − 200)/800 = 30 × 0.75 = 22.5 mice per year Problem B: population at a given time. Bacteria start at N₀ = 10 with K = 1000 and r = 0.5 per hour. What's N after 8 hours? (K − N₀)/N₀ = 99 N(8) = 1000 / (1 + 99 · e^(−4)) = 1000 / 2.8132 ≈ 355.46 (Check this against the Experiment 0 table: it matches.) Problem C: time to a target. How long until the same bacteria reach 90% of K? Solving N = 0.9K gives a general formula for reaching a fraction p of the ceiling: t = (1/r) · ln( p/(1−p) · (K − N₀)/N₀ ) For p = 0.9: t = 2 · ln(9 × 99) = 2 · ln(891) ≈ 13.58 hours . That's the value in the r = 0.5 row of Experiment 1. Experiment 6: break the simulator This one is for anyone who's ever written a simulation loop. At r = 0.5 , Euler with Δt = 1 is visibly slower than the truth (305 vs 476 at t = 9). The step is too coarse. At r = 1.5 , Euler overshoots K (1002) before settling. At r = 2.2 , Euler never settles. It bounces between about 1,163 and 747 forever , while the true curve flattens smoothly at 1,000. That bouncing is a numerical artifact, not biology. The stepping scheme is only stable when r·Δt < 2. Past that, the discrete update flips the sign of the error each step. (Researchers actually study discrete logistic maps for this very behavior, but that's a different model than the continuous one here.) The takeaway for anyone building or choosing a growth simulator: use the closed-form solution when you have one. According to its documentation, the carrying capacity tool evaluates the integrated formula at every time step rather than stepping iteratively, so you don't get these step-size artifacts, whatever r you enter. Exponential and logistic curves look almost the same before the population nears half of K. You can only pin down K well once the data bends toward the plateau. If you ever read a paper or a homework answer claiming a confident K from the exponential phase alone, be skeptical. What the model assumes (and where it breaks) Every result above depends on the logistic model's assumptions: K and r are constants. In reality, K shifts with seasons, climate, and habitat change. A drought lowers K for a grazing population. Instant feedback. The brake responds to current density with no delay (Experiment 4's limitation). All individuals are identical. No age structure, no sex ratio, no differences in competitive ability. A closed population. No immigration or emigration. Smooth, continuous growth. Real populations are made of whole individuals, and small populations face random chance. None of this makes the model useless. It makes it a baseline , the thing you compare real data against to see how a population deviates and why . What actually sets K? The ceiling comes from limiting factors . Ecologists split them into two groups: Density-dependent factors get worse as the population grows. Competition for food, water, space, and light; predation; disease spreading in crowded groups; and in a closed system like a yeast flask, the build-up of toxic waste such as ethanol. These are what produce the braking effect in the equation. Density-independent factors hit regardless of population size: wildfires, hurricanes, severe weather. They knock N down temporarily, after which the population regrows along the S-curve. They don't define K itself. Five misconceptions this sandbox cleans up "Growth is fastest at K." No: growth is zero at K and fastest at K/2 . "A higher growth rate means a higher carrying capacity." No: r controls speed, K controls the ceiling (Experiment 1). "A bigger starting population means a bigger final population." No: N₀ shifts timing only (Experiment 2). "At K, nothing is happening." Births still equal deaths. The population is in dynamic balance, not frozen. "Populations always settle smoothly at K." Only in the basic model. Real populations overshoot, crash, and oscillate, especially with time lags.
