# How to Structure a WordPress Site So Google (and Visitors) Understand It

## Overview

Most WordPress sites do not have a traffic problem — they have a structure problem. Pages are added one at a time, menus grow without a plan, and after a year the site is a maze: Google cannot tell which page matters most, and visitors cannot find the service they came for. A clear structure fixes both problems at once, and it costs nothing except a little planning. Think of your site as a small shop, not a warehouse. A shop has a front door (the homepage), a few clear sections (services, about, contact), and shelves inside each section. A warehouse has everything piled together. Your homepage should link to your main service pages, and each service page should link back to the homepage and sideways to related services. When every page links to every other page in a giant footer menu, nothing stands out — Google treats all pages as equally important, which means none of them is. Start with five to eight main pages and no more. For a typical small business in Pakistan, that means: home, two to four service pages (one per real service, not one page listing everything), about, blog, and contact. If you offer ten services, group them: "Web Design," "SEO," and "Maintenance" as main pages, with the details underneath. A visitor who lands on your SEO page should immediately see what SEO includes, who it is for, and how to enquire — not a wall of text about your whole company. URLs matter more than people think. A clean URL like /website-development-services/ tells both Google and a human what the page is about. A URL full of numbers, dates, and random words tells nobody anything. When you create a page in WordPress, check its permalink before publishing, and keep it short, readable, and stable — changing URLs later breaks links and loses rankings. Internal links are the quiet ranking tool most small sites ignore. Every new blog post should link to the one or two service pages it relates to, using natural words as the link text — not "click here." Over months, those links tell Google which pages you consider most important, and they guide real visitors from reading an article to making an enquiry. That is the whole point of a blog: not traffic for its own sake, but a path from useful content to a service page. Finally, prune. If a page has had no visits and no purpose for a year, it is not harmless — it dilutes the rest of the site. Either update it with a real purpose or remove it and redirect it to the closest live page. A smaller, well-organised site almost always outperforms a larger, messy one.
