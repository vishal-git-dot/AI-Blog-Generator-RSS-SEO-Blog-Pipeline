# Government Form Photos Keep Getting Rejected? The Size Rules, Finally Explained

## Overview

One form wants 600 × 600 pixels. The next wants a photo "under 50 KB". A third measures in millimetres. If you have ever had an application photo rejected and stared at the error message wondering what you did wrong — you are not alone, and it is probably not your photo. It is the patchwork of size rules behind it. I run a small site of free photo and document tools, and form-photo rejections are the single most common problem people write to us about. Here is the pattern, because once you see it, the numbers stop looking random. Why every form seems to want something different Each system was built at a different time, for a different kind of checking: A US consular system measures your photo in pixels , because software verifies it before a person does. An exam board cares about file weight , because hundreds of thousands of applicants uploading heavy photos would bury its servers. A printed passport photo is measured in inches or millimetres , because it will be cut out and glued onto paper. Three kinds of measurement — pixels, print size, file size. Learn what each one means and which forms use which, and no application will surprise you again. The sizes that come up again and again 600 × 600 pixels, under 240 KB — the US digital standard. The DV Lottery, the DS-160 visa form and most US online applications use exactly this. Square, JPEG, taken within the last 6 months, with your head filling 50–69% of the frame. 2 × 2 inches (51 × 51 mm) — the printed US passport photo. Same head rules as above, delivered as a print rather than a file. When a form asks for a digital version at 600 × 600 or larger, it is usually this photo in file form. 35 × 45 mm — the Schengen and European standard , also used for many other countries' visas and ID cards. Slightly taller than it is wide, which is why a square US photo gets rejected by European systems. Exact-KB caps — the portal standard. Many application portals skip pixels entirely and just cap the file: a photo at 50 KB, a signature at 20 KB, a supporting PDF at 200 KB. The photo can be perfect and still fail if the file is 51 KB instead of 50. The full breakdown, form by form, is in our guide: Government Form Photo Size Requirements . The order that actually works Most rejections happen because people compress first and crop second — and every crop changes the file size again. Do it the other way: Crop and frame first. Get the head size and the pixel/print dimensions right. Check the framing against the form's rules before touching the file size. Our free Form Photo Validator does this check and auto-fixes the common failures. Compress to the exact KB last. If the cap is 50 KB, resize the photo to exactly 50 KB with a tool that targets a number, not a percentage — like Resize Photo to 50KB . Signatures have their own cap: Resize Signature to 20KB . Two more traps worth knowing: A photo taken on an iPhone may be HEIC, not JPEG. Some portals reject it silently. Convert it first: HEIC to JPG . Printed photos start as digital ones. For a US passport photo you print at home, start from the US Passport Photo Maker so the 2 × 2 print matches the digital rules too. Several photos, one upload? Many portals want a single PDF at the end. Image to PDF puts each photo on its own page, in your order. None of this is hard once the pattern is clear. The forms are not testing your photography — they are testing whether the file matches three numbers. Match the numbers, in the right order, and the rejection emails stop.
