# The language token tax: why your non-English prompts cost more, and what to do about it

## Overview

AI APIs don't bill you per word. They bill you per token , and a token is whatever the model's tokenizer decided a piece of text should be. That sounds like a detail until you count. Here's one instruction, "Always answer in JSON format.", on GPT-4o's tokenizer: English: 6 tokens Hindi: 9 tokens Punjabi: 18 tokens Same meaning, three times the price. I've started calling this the language token tax , and if you ship LLM features outside English, you're paying it on every call. Tokens are not words Modern tokenizers learn their vocabulary from training data. They see a huge amount of English, so common English words become single tokens: answer , format , always . Languages that show up less often in that data don't get that treatment. Their words are split into smaller pieces, sometimes down to single characters or raw bytes. More pieces means more tokens, and more tokens means a bigger bill and less room in the context window. I measured it in 55 languages tokensaver , the open-source prompt-cost tool I maintain, has a test that runs the same sentence in 55 languages. So I counted every one of them with o200k_base , the tokenizer behind GPT-4o and OpenAI's other current models: Language Tokens vs. English English 6 1.0× Spanish 7 1.2× French, Russian, Persian, Chinese (Simplified) 8 1.3× German, Arabic, Hindi, Japanese 9 1.5× Korean, Swahili 10 1.7× Bengali 11 1.8× Tamil, Burmese 14 2.3× Telugu 17 2.8× Punjabi (Gurmukhi) 18 3.0× 36 of the 55 languages needed at least 1.5× as many tokens as English. Two practical takeaways: If you're still on an older model, the tax may be several times bigger than you think. Moving to a newer model can cut tokens for non-English prompts even before you touch the prompt. Every provider tokenizes differently. Claude and Gemini use their own tokenizers, and there's no offline way to count them exactly. Measure on the model you actually use. A word of caution: this is one short sentence. Ratios move around with the text, so treat these numbers as a direction, not a price list. Before you budget a product in another language, count your own prompts in that language. What you can't fix, and what you can You can't change how a tokenizer splits Tamil. But a lot of what makes prompts expensive has nothing to do with language: Pretty-printed JSON. Indentation and line breaks cost tokens in every script. Duplicated rules. A rule pasted twice is paid for twice, in any language. Extra whitespace. Trailing spaces and runs of blank lines. These are exactly the fixes that work everywhere, so I made sure tokensaver applies them in any language. I tested it on three sample prompts with that kind of waste: one each in English, Persian and Chinese. Here are the results on GPT-4o with tokensaver optimize --apply all : Prompt Before After Saved English 486 386 −20.6% Persian 350 259 −26.0% Chinese (Simplified) 273 189 −30.8% The non-English prompts saved more , in percentage terms. When every word costs more tokens, removing a duplicated paragraph removes more tokens too. Safe changes, like minifying JSON, are applied automatically; tokensaver re-parses the JSON to make sure the data didn't change. Anything that could change meaning, like removing a duplicate, waits for you to review it. It runs fully offline, so your prompts never leave your machine. To see what the same prompt costs across models, cheapest first: tokensaver compare prompt.txt Filler is language-specific, so it needs native speakers One kind of waste is tied to language: polite filler. "I would like you to please…" or "Thanks in advance!" costs tokens and changes nothing about what the model does. You can't write those rules from a dictionary. You need someone who knows which phrases are truly empty, and which ones only look empty. This week, a native Persian speaker contributed exactly that. tokensaver now removes «لطفاً» ("please"), «از تو می‌خواهم که…» ("I want you to…") and «ممنون می‌شوم اگر…» ("I'd appreciate it if…"). It handles the different ways people actually type them, with or without the invisible zero-width joiner in the middle of a word. Just as interesting is what we left out : «اگر امکان دارد», "if possible". It looks like filler, but "if possible, also write tests" is a real condition. Delete it and an optional request becomes mandatory. That's the kind of call only a native speaker can make with confidence. Your language next? The filler rule now understands English and Persian. TL;DR You pay per token, and most non-English text needs more tokens for the same meaning: 1.5× or more for 36 of 55 languages in my test, up to 3× for Punjabi. Newer tokenizers are much fairer than older ones, so check which one your model uses. You can't fix the tokenizer, but JSON minification, duplicate removal and whitespace cleanup work in any language. On my sample prompts they cut 26% in Persian and 31% in Chinese.
