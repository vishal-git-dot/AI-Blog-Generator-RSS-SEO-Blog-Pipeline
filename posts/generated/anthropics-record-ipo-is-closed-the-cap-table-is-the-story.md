# Anthropic's record IPO is closed. The cap table is the story

## Overview

The biggest initial public offering on record, per the Associated Press, is now done. Today Anthropic announced the closing of its IPO: 1.127 billion Class A shares at $83 each, including the underwriters' full option for 147 million more, for gross proceeds of about $93.5 billion. Roughly $91.6 billion went to the company; the rest was sold by existing stockholders, whose proceeds Anthropic did not receive. The shares began trading on Nasdaq yesterday under the symbol ANT. The Associated Press reports that the $83 pricing valued the company at $1.5 trillion and surpassed SpaceX as the largest public offering ever, and that the stock surged in its first day of trading. The more instructive number is smaller. Alongside the IPO, Anthropic closed a concurrent private placement of about 313 million Class N shares, also at $83, worth roughly $26 billion. The buyers were not pension funds. They were Advanced Micro Devices, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, NVIDIA and TSMC Partners - the chip designers, memory makers, cloud providers and manufacturer that sell Anthropic the compute it runs on. In total, the offering and placement raised about $119.5 billion in gross proceeds. Read that cap table as a leadership signal. The companies that supply frontier AI now own a piece of it, and a frontier lab's biggest capital relationship is with its own vendors. None of this is hidden; it is in the company's own announcement. But it changes the questions a buyer should ask. When your model provider's shareholders are also its suppliers, capacity, pricing and roadmap decisions sit inside that circle. Enterprises planning multi-year commitments on any frontier platform should treat vendor ownership structure as part of due diligence, the same way they already treat data terms and uptime. There is a second test ahead, and it is governance. Anthropic is organized as a public benefit corporation and has built its brand on safety research and cautious deployment. As of this week it also answers to public shareholders and a quarterly earnings calendar. Whether a safety-first mission survives contact with public-market expectations is now an empirical question, not a debating point. Leaders choosing AI platforms on stated values should watch what the company files and does, not what it says on its about page. The same week delivered a quieter confirmation of where enterprise AI strategy is heading. Palo Alto Networks announced a definitive agreement to acquire Applied Compute, a platform for post-training and inference of open-weight models, in an all-stock deal valued at $4.5 billion. The acquirer's stated thesis: the enterprise future is hybrid, mixing open-weight and closed frontier models, and no large provider delivers the open side securely today. Applied Compute's CEO, Yash Patil, is set to become Palo Alto Networks' Co-Chief AI Officer when the deal closes, which is expected in the company's fiscal third quarter of 2027, subject to regulatory clearance. When the biggest enterprise security vendor spends $4.5 billion on open-weight plumbing, the build-versus-buy debate inside most companies is effectively over: the answer is both, secured. Two caveats belong with this week's excitement. The AP notes the debut came amid market nerves, including worries about an AI bubble, and a first-day surge says nothing about where the stock trades in a year. And a $4.5 billion acquisition agreement is not a closed deal or a delivered product. The durable takeaway is structural. AI's center of gravity this week moved from model announcements to money and ownership: record public capital for a frontier lab, supplier-shareholders inside its cap table, and an incumbent paying billions to secure the open-weight side of the enterprise stack. Leaders who only track benchmark scores are missing the part of the market that will shape pricing, capacity and leverage for years.
