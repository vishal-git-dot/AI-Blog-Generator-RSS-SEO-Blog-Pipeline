# Tripadvisor Expects SEO Headwinds to Persist Into 2027

## Overview

Tripadvisor expects SEO-related pressure on growth to continue into 2027, a notable signal from one of travel search’s largest established brands. The company’s third-quarter 2026 results connect a sustained decline in traffic from changes in the search landscape with a major goodwill impairment, while its executives also pointed to continuing SEO headwinds and a greater reliance on paid channels. For travel publishers, marketplaces and advertisers, the message is less about one ranking change than the economics of organic discovery. A site can retain a substantial brand and still face a prolonged shift in how users reach travel information. What Tripadvisor reported In its third-quarter 2026 financial results filed with the SEC , Tripadvisor reported revenue of $436.4 million for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, down 11% year over year. It reported a GAAP net loss of $316.9 million. That loss was driven largely by a $369.5 million non-cash goodwill impairment charge in the Hotels and Other segment. Tripadvisor attributed the impairment to a sustained decline in traffic from changes in the search landscape. An impairment is an accounting measure, not a direct count of lost organic clicks. Still, it is a material indication that the company believes the change has affected the expected value of assets in that business. The company’s marketing costs also took a larger share of continuing revenue. Marketing represented 48.8% of continuing revenue in Q3 2026, compared with 43.3% in the prior-year quarter. Tripadvisor said its transition toward paid channels contributed to the increase. The supplied research does not define every component of that activity, so this figure should not be read as a universal measure of organic traffic, clicks or revenue. It does, however, establish that SEO remains commercially significant for Tripadvisor even as the company adjusts its channel mix. Noonan also said SEO headwinds would persist into 2027. The company expects a future tailwind only once it can mechanically exit the headwind. That framing matters because it does not present the issue as a short-lived seasonal fluctuation or an immediately solved optimization problem. Search Engine Journal’s reporting on the earnings discussion noted that SEO headwinds created an approximate 3% drag on Experiences bookings. Earlier in the year, the drag was reportedly higher in August, at about five percentage points, before moderating. These figures concern Tripadvisor’s reported business performance and should not be generalized to every travel website or to search as a whole. Why this matters beyond Tripadvisor Travel search has long depended on informational pages that help people compare destinations, hotels, restaurants and things to do. Changes in search-result presentation, user journeys or referral patterns can therefore affect not only rankings, but also the volume and quality of visitors arriving at a travel site. Tripadvisor’s results are a reminder that organic visibility is not the same as a business outcome. A brand may still rank for valuable queries while receiving fewer visits, weaker booking intent or less traffic than its historical operating model assumed. If recovering that demand requires paid acquisition, marketing costs can rise even when revenue does not. The disclosure does not isolate a single cause, platform or search feature. It should not be treated as proof that AI-generated answers alone caused Tripadvisor’s traffic decline. The company referred broadly to changes in the search landscape. That uncertainty is important: search visibility planning should measure the actual path from search exposure to visits and conversions rather than assign all change to one feature. What travel teams should measure now The practical response is not to abandon SEO. It is to make organic search exposure easier to evaluate alongside bookings, revenue and paid acquisition costs . Travel businesses and publishers can focus on a few connected questions: Which query groups still deliver visits that progress to bookings, inquiries or other meaningful actions? Where have impressions, rankings, click-through rates and landing-page sessions changed in different directions? How much demand is being replaced through paid channels, and at what marginal cost? Which content types are most dependent on search referrals, such as destination guides, hotel pages or experience listings? Are brand mentions and citations in AI-driven discovery experiences adding reach that conventional referral reporting may not fully show? This approach helps separate a ranking problem from a click problem, a conversion problem or a broader change in demand. It also makes it easier to set realistic forecasting assumptions.
